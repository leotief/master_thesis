\section{Variance Analysis}

\subsection{Conditional Sampling MC Estimator}

The MC estimator draws $N$ samples ${\xm}^{(i)} \sim p(\xm \mid \xo)$ and returns
\[
  \widehat{p}_{\mathrm{MC}}(N) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \sigma\!\left(w^T x^{(i)}\right).
\]
Since $\sigma(\cdot) \in (0,1)$, the per-sample variance $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MC}}
:= \mathrm{Var}_{p(\xm\mid\xo)}\!\left[\sigma(w^T x)\right]$ is bounded by $1/4$,
giving
\[
  \mathrm{Var}\!\left[\widehat{p}_{\mathrm{MC}}(N)\right]
  = \frac{\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MC}}}{N}
  \;\leq\; \frac{1}{4N}.
\]
The $1/N$ factor follows from the standard variance-of-a-sample-mean identity:
since the draws are i.i.d., the variance of their average is the per-sample
variance divided by $N$.
The bound $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MC}} \leq 1/4$ holds because $\sigma(\cdot) \in (0,1)$:
for any $[0,1]$-valued random variable $Y$,
\[
  \mathrm{Var}(Y) = \E[Y^2] - \E[Y]^2 \leq \E[Y] - \E[Y]^2 = \E[Y](1-\E[Y]) \leq \tfrac{1}{4},
\]
where the first inequality uses $Y^2 \leq Y$ on $[0,1]$ and the second uses
$p(1-p) \leq 1/4$ for all $p \in [0,1]$, with equality at $p = 1/2$.

\subsection{PCPG-MC Estimator}

The PCPG-MC estimator with $M$ outer (PG) and $L$ inner (Gaussian) samples returns
\[
  \widehat{p}_{\mathrm{PCPG}}(M,L)
  = \frac{1}{2ML}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{L}
    \mathrm{Re}\!\left[h(\gamma_j, u_{ij})\right],
\]
where
$h(\gamma, u) := \exp\!\left(\tfrac{1}{8\gamma} + iu\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2\gamma}-c\right)\right)
\cdot \phi_{\xm\mid\xo}(-uw_m)$,
$c = w_o^T\xo$, and $u_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\gamma_j)$.

\subsubsection{Output range}
A single sample ($M{=}L{=}1$) returns $\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re}[h(\gamma,u)]$.

The characteristic function $\phi(s) = \E[e^{isx}]$ satisfies $|\phi(s)| \leq 1$
for all $s$.

Since $|h(\gamma,u)| = e^{1/(8\gamma)} \cdot |\phi(-uw_m)| \leq e^{1/(8\gamma)}$,
the value lies in $\left[-\tfrac{1}{2}e^{1/(8\gamma)},\, +\tfrac{1}{2}e^{1/(8\gamma)}\right]$.

Because $\gamma \sim \PG(1,0)$ can be arbitrarily small, $e^{1/(8\gamma)}$ is
unbounded, so individual PCPG samples are not confined to $[0,1]$ and can be
negative or exceed 1.

By contrast, every individual MC sample lies in $(0,1)$.

\subsubsection{Variance}

The PCPG-MC estimator has two nested levels of randomness.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Outer level}: $M$ independent draws $\gamma_j \sim \PG(1,0)$.
  \item \textbf{Inner level}: for each $j$, $L$ independent draws $u_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \gamma_j)$.
\end{enumerate}
Let $Z = \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re}[h(\gamma, u)]$ denote a single ``raw'' sample.
The estimator is the mean of $ML$ such values, grouped so that each outer sample
$\gamma_j$ drives a block of $L$ inner values.

\paragraph{Step 1: variance within one outer block.}
Fix $\gamma_j$.  The $L$ inner samples $u_{ij}$ are i.i.d.\ $\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma_j)$, so the
block average $\bar Z_j := \frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L} Z_{ij}$
has conditional variance $\sigma^2_{A,\gamma_j} / L$, where
$\sigma^2_{A,\gamma} := \mathrm{Var}_{u \mid \gamma}[Z \mid \gamma]$.

\paragraph{Step 2: law of total variance for $\bar Z_j$.}
The $M$ blocks are independent, so it suffices to study a single block.
Applying the law of total variance across the two levels of randomness:
\[
  \mathrm{Var}[\bar Z_j]
  = \underbrace{\E_\gamma\!\left[\mathrm{Var}[\bar Z_j \mid \gamma_j]\right]}_{\text{avg.\ inner variance}}
  +\underbrace{\mathrm{Var}_\gamma\!\left[\E[\bar Z_j \mid \gamma_j]\right]}_{\text{outer PG variance}}
  = \frac{\sigma^2_A}{L} + \sigma^2_B,
\]
where
\[
  \sigma^2_A := \E_\gamma\bigl[\sigma^2_{A,\gamma}\bigr]
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \sigma^2_B := \mathrm{Var}_\gamma\!\left[\E_{u \mid \gamma}[Z \mid \gamma]\right].
\]
$\sigma^2_A$ measures how much the inner Gaussian integral fluctuates for a typical PG draw.
$\sigma^2_B$ measures how much the PG draw $\gamma$ itself shifts the result.

\paragraph{Step 3: overall variance.}
The estimator is $\widehat p = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\bar Z_j$ with $M$ i.i.d.\ blocks, so
\[
  \mathrm{Var}\!\left[\widehat{p}_{\mathrm{PCPG}}(M,L)\right]
  = \frac{1}{M}\,\mathrm{Var}[\bar Z_j]
  = \frac{\sigma^2_A}{ML} + \frac{\sigma^2_B}{M}.
\]

\paragraph{Step 4: fixed-ratio budget.}
Writing the total budget as $N = ML$ and the inner/outer ratio as $r = L/M$
(so $M = \sqrt{N/r}$):
\[
  \mathrm{Var}\!\left[\widehat{p}_{\mathrm{PCPG}}(M,L)\right]
  = \frac{\sigma^2_A}{N} + \frac{\sigma^2_B\,\sqrt{r}}{\sqrt{N}}.
\]
The first term decays as $O(1/N)$, the same rate as MC.
The second term decays only as $O(1/\sqrt{N})$, which is \emph{slower}.
For large $N$ the second term dominates, so PCPG-MC will have higher variance
than MC whenever $\sigma^2_B > 0$.

\paragraph{Why $\sigma^2_A$ can be large.}
The problem comes from completing the square in Trick~2, which introduced the
factors $e^{1/(8\gamma)}$ and $e^{iu/(2\gamma)}$ into the integrand. Both depend on
$1/\gamma$ and become extreme for small $\gamma$.

To see what happens, fix $z$. The inner expectation is then known exactly from the
HS identity,
\[
  \E_{u \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\gamma)}\!\left[ e^{-i\left(z - \frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)u} \right]
  = \exp\!\left( -\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma \left( z - \tfrac{1}{2\gamma} \right)^2 \right)
  \approx e^{-1/(8\gamma)} \quad \text{for small } \gamma,
\]
which is tiny. Multiplied by $e^{1/(8\gamma)}$ it gives a value of order one. So
the inner average has to produce a tiny number from terms of modulus one that
almost cancel, and every bit of noise in that average is amplified by
$e^{1/(8\gamma)}$. For $\gamma = 0.01$ this factor is already about
$2.7 \cdot 10^{5}$.

In terms of variance, a single inner sample has modulus $e^{1/(8\gamma)}$, so its
second moment is $e^{1/(4\gamma)}$. Near zero the density of $\PG(1,0)$ decays
only like $e^{-1/(8\gamma)}$, which is not fast enough to compensate, so averaged
over $\gamma$ the second moment is infinite: formally $\sigma^2_A = \infty$.
Small values of $\gamma$ are rare, which is why the estimator still works in
practice, but when they occur they produce extreme samples, and a moderately
large $L$ is needed to suppress the inner noise.

\paragraph{Optimal budget split.}
Looking at the variance formula from Step~3,
\[
  \mathrm{Var} = \frac{\sigma^2_A}{ML} + \frac{\sigma^2_B}{M},
\]
the two terms respond to the budget differently.
The first term shrinks when $L$ is large (many inner samples per PG draw).
The second term shrinks when $M$ is large (many PG draws).
Since $N = ML$ is fixed, making $L$ larger forces $M$ smaller and vice versa ---
you cannot reduce both terms simultaneously.

Substituting $M = N/L$ and minimising over $L$ at fixed $N$ gives the optimal split:
\[
  L^* = \sqrt{\frac{N\,\sigma^2_A}{\sigma^2_B}},
  \qquad
  M^* = \sqrt{\frac{N\,\sigma^2_B}{\sigma^2_A}},
  \qquad
  r^* := \frac{L^*}{M^*} = \frac{\sigma^2_A}{\sigma^2_B}.
\]
In words: use more inner samples than outer samples if and only if $\sigma^2_A > \sigma^2_B$.
Because the inner variance is typically much larger (the $e^{1/(8\gamma)}$ blowup), the
recommended ratio is $r = 4$, meaning four inner samples per PG draw.
At $N = 1000$ and $r = 4$ this gives $M^* \approx 16$ outer draws and $L^* \approx 63$
inner samples per draw; equal splitting ($M = L = 32$) would leave the large inner
variance insufficiently averaged.

The minimum variance at the optimal split is $2\sqrt{\sigma^2_A\sigma^2_B}/\sqrt{N}$,
which is still $O(1/\sqrt{N})$.
No choice of $r$ removes the $O(1/\sqrt{N})$ scaling as long as $\sigma^2_B > 0$.

\paragraph{When does $\sigma^2_B$ become small?}
Recall that $\sigma^2_B = \mathrm{Var}_\gamma\!\left[\E_{u\mid\gamma}[Z\mid\gamma]\right]$:
it measures how much the value of the inner integral shifts as $\gamma$ changes.
For a GMM with diagonal covariance, the CF of the missing features satisfies
\[
  \bigl|\phi_{\xm\mid\xo}(-uw_m)\bigr|
  \;\leq\;
  \exp\!\left(-\frac{u^2}{2}\,D_m\right),
  \qquad
  D_m := \sum_{j \in \mathrm{mis}} w_j^2\,\sigma_{kj}^2,
\]
where $D_m$ collects the variance of the missing part of $w^T x$.
When $D_m$ is large, the CF decays very fast in $u$: the integrand $h(\gamma,u)$
is nearly zero unless $|u|$ is small.
For any fixed $\gamma$, the relevant $u$ values are of order $\sqrt{\gamma}$;
large $D_m$ means even moderately large $\gamma$ is enough to zero out the integrand.
As a result, the inner expectation $\E_{u\mid\gamma}[Z\mid\gamma]$ becomes nearly
independent of $\gamma$ --- it is close to zero for a wide range of $\gamma$ values
and hardly varies --- so $\sigma^2_B \approx 0$.

\paragraph{Effect of missing ratio (fixed total dimension).}
Increasing the fraction of missing features (larger $d_m$, same $d$) adds more terms
to the sum defining $D_m$, directly increasing it.
The CF damps more strongly, $\sigma^2_B$ decreases, and PCPG-MC recovers closer to
$O(1/N)$ convergence.  MC behaves in the opposite way: more missing features make
$w^T x$ more random, increasing $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MC}}$.
The PCPG family therefore \emph{gains} relative to MC as the missing ratio grows.

\paragraph{Effect of total dimension (fixed missing ratio).}
Increasing $d$ while keeping the missing ratio $\rho = d_m/d$ constant grows both
$d_m$ and $d_o$ proportionally.
The additional terms in $D_m$ are exactly offset by the fact that logistic-regression
weights $w_j$ shrink with larger $d$ (the total discriminative power is spread over
more features), so $D_m$ stays roughly constant and the CF damping does not improve.
At fixed missing ratio, the rate improvement for PCPG-MC described above does
\emph{not} automatically follow from adding more features; what matters is the
missing-feature energy $D_m$, not the total dimension alone.

\paragraph{Comparison.}
\[
  \mathrm{Var}\!\left[\widehat{p}_{\mathrm{MC}}(N)\right] = \frac{\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MC}}}{N}
  \;\leq\; \frac{1}{4N},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{Var}\!\left[\widehat{p}_{\mathrm{PCPG}}(M,L)\right]
  = \frac{\sigma^2_A}{N} + \frac{\sigma^2_B\sqrt{r}}{\sqrt{N}}.
\]
For low-missing-rate settings where $\sigma^2_B$ is non-negligible, PCPG-MC
is less sample-efficient than MC.
The $O(1/\sqrt{N})$ slowdown motivates replacing the inner MC with
Gauss-Hermite quadrature (PCPG-GH).


\subsection{PCPG-GH Estimator}

\subsubsection{Variance}

The PCPG-GH estimator keeps the $M$ outer PG draws $\gamma_j \sim \PG(1,0)$ but
replaces each inner MC average with a $n_H$-point Gauss-Hermite rule.
Because the inner quadrature is \emph{deterministic} given $\gamma_j$, the inner
noise term $\sigma^2_A/L$ vanishes entirely.
The only remaining source of variance is the outer PG draw:
\[
  \mathrm{Var}\!\left[\widehat{p}_{\mathrm{GH}}(M)\right]
  = \frac{\sigma^2_B}{M}.
\]
The total evaluation budget is $N = M\cdot n_H$ (each outer sample evaluates the CF
at $n_H$ frequencies), so
\[
  \mathrm{Var}\!\left[\widehat{p}_{\mathrm{GH}}(M)\right]
  = \frac{\sigma^2_B\,n_H}{N},
\]
which is $O(1/N)$, matching the MC rate and eliminating the slow $O(1/\sqrt{N})$
term that limits PCPG-MC.
The factor $n_H$ is a fixed constant: with $n_H = 20$ nodes the per-budget
variance is $20\,\sigma^2_B$, which is favourable whenever $\sigma^2_B$ is small
compared to $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MC}}/n_H$.

In short, PCPG-GH is a strictly better choice than PCPG-MC at the same outer
budget whenever $\sigma^2_A$ is non-negligible, because replacing the inner MC
with quadrature costs nothing in outer samples and eliminates the dominant noise
source.

The problem does not disappear completely, however; it turns from variance into
quadrature error. With $u = \sqrt{2\gamma}\,t$ the inner integrand contains the
factor $\exp\!\left(-i\left(z - \tfrac{1}{2\gamma}\right)\sqrt{2\gamma}\,t\right)$,
which oscillates with frequency $\left|z - \tfrac{1}{2\gamma}\right|\sqrt{2\gamma}$
in the Hermite variable $t$. This frequency is large when $\gamma$ is small, but
also when $|w^T x|$ is large. A fixed number of nodes cannot resolve fast
oscillations, and the exact inner value
$\exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma(z - \tfrac{1}{2\gamma})^2)$ it should produce is then
tiny, so the rule has to sum terms of modulus one to almost zero. Any error in
that sum is multiplied by $e^{1/(8\gamma)}$, which also amplifies rounding errors
(in single precision, with relative accuracy $\approx 10^{-7}$, only about two
digits are left at $\gamma = 0.01$). The CF damping helps when many features are
missing, because it suppresses large $|u|$; with few missing features and a
confident prediction, the inner quadrature error can become significant.

\subsection{PCPG-Quad Estimator}

Replacing both integrals with quadrature (Golub--Welsch for the outer PG
integral, Gauss--Hermite for the inner) makes the estimator fully deterministic:
\[
  \mathrm{Var}\!\left[\widehat{p}_{\mathrm{Quad}}\right] = 0.
\]
The estimator always returns the same value for the same inputs, and its
output is bounded since the PG quadrature nodes $\gamma_j$ are fixed
positive numbers --- the factor $e^{1/(8\gamma_j)}$ is a known finite
constant for each node.
The error is purely from quadrature approximation (bias), not variance.
The amplification by $e^{1/(8\gamma)}$ is mild here, because the smallest
Golub--Welsch node is not close to zero ($\gamma_{\min} \approx 0.046$ for 19
nodes, giving $e^{1/(8\gamma_{\min})} \approx 15$). The oscillation problem of
the inner Gauss--Hermite rule for large $|w^T x|$ remains, since the inner rule is
the same as in PCPG-GH.

\section{Bias Analysis}

\subsection{MC Estimator}
MC is unbiased for all $N \geq 1$: sampling from $p(\xm \mid \xo)$ and
averaging $\sigma(w^T x)$ is a direct Monte Carlo estimate of the target
expectation, so $\E[\widehat{p}_{\mathrm{MC}}(N)] = p(y \mid \xo)$ exactly.

\subsection{PCPG-MC Estimator}
PCPG-MC is also unbiased. Both integrals are approximated by ordinary MC,
so by linearity of expectation the estimator equals the true double integral,
which is $p(y \mid \xo)$ by the PG-sigmoid and Hubbard--Stratonovich
identities.

\subsection{PCPG-GH Estimator}
The outer PG integral is still MC (unbiased). The inner integral is replaced
by Gauss--Hermite quadrature, which introduces a small approximation error.
For $N_H$ quadrature nodes, GH integrates polynomials of degree up to $2N_H - 1$
exactly, and for smooth (analytic) integrands the error decays
super-algebraically in $N_H$. In our setting the integrand is analytic in $u$,
so the bias is negligible already for small $N_H$ (in practice $N_H \geq 20$
is sufficient).

\subsection{PCPG-Quad Estimator}
Both integrals introduce a quadrature error. The Gauss--Hermite inner error
is the same as for PCPG-GH. The Golub--Welsch PG quadrature with $M$ nodes
integrates polynomials in $\gamma$ exactly up to degree $2M-1$; for smooth
functions of $\gamma$ the error again decays rapidly with $M$. The maximum
reliable order is $M \leq 19$ (limited by numerical precision in the moment
computation). At $M = 19$ and $N_H \geq 20$, both errors are numerically
negligible and the output closely matches the unbiased MC ground truth.
This holds for moderate $|w^T x|$. For large positive $w^T x$ the outer error is
amplified by $e^{z/2}$, which the sign flip from Section~\ref{subsec:sign-flip}
removes; with the flip, the outer rule fitted directly to the sigmoid
(Section~\ref{subsubsec:sigmoid-fit}) is more accurate than Golub--Welsch with
fewer nodes. The inner Gauss--Hermite error for large $|w^T x|$ is not affected
by the flip.

\paragraph{Bias.}
MC and PCPG-MC are exactly unbiased. PCPG-GH and PCPG-Quad introduce small
quadrature errors that are negligible at the parameter choices used
($n_H \geq 20$, $M_{\mathrm{PG}} \leq 19$).

\paragraph{Variance and convergence rate.}
MC achieves $O(1/N)$ variance, bounded above by $1/(4N)$.
PCPG-GH also achieves $O(1/N)$, but the effective constant is $\sigma^2_B\,n_H$:
it is preferable to MC when $\sigma^2_B$ is small, which happens precisely when
many features are missing and the CF damps strongly.
PCPG-MC is the only estimator with a slower $O(1/\sqrt{N})$ rate; the outer PG
variance $\sigma^2_B$ introduces a $\sigma^2_B\sqrt{r}/\sqrt{N}$ term that
persists regardless of how the budget is split between $M$ and $L$.
In the high-missing-data regime $\sigma^2_B \approx 0$ and PCPG-MC recovers
$O(1/N)$, but in that regime PCPG-GH is still at least as good.
PCPG-Quad has zero variance by construction; its ``error'' is pure bias from
finite-order quadrature, which decays exponentially in the number of nodes and
is negligible above $n_H = 20$ and $M_{\mathrm{PG}} = 19$.

\paragraph{When to use which.}
MC is the simplest and most reliable choice when sampling from $p(\xm \mid \xo)$
is easy and low variance is the priority.
PCPG-GH is the natural first upgrade: it eliminates the inner MC noise at no extra
outer-sample cost and always achieves $O(1/N)$; use it whenever evaluating the CF
is cheaper than drawing samples.
PCPG-MC should be preferred over PCPG-GH only when the CF is cheap but computing
the Gauss-Hermite nodes is costly, or as a reference implementation to isolate
$\sigma^2_A$ empirically.
PCPG-Quad is the right choice when a fully deterministic, reproducible result
is required --- for example when differentiating through the estimator or when
multiple evaluations at the same point must agree exactly.


\section{Effect of Dimensionality}
\label{sec:dimensionality}

Two distinct quantities can vary: the number of missing features $d_m$ at fixed
total dimension $d$ (equivalently, the missing ratio $\rho = d_m/d$), and the
total dimension $d$ itself at a fixed ratio $\rho$.
Their effects are different and we treat them separately.
The key quantity that links both estimators is the \emph{missing-feature energy}
\[
  D_m := \sum_{j \in \mathrm{mis}} w_j^2\,\sigma_{kj}^2,
\]
which measures how much variance the uncertain part of $w^T x$ contributes.

\paragraph{Effect of the missing ratio (more missing features, fixed $d$).}
As $d_m$ grows at fixed $d$, more terms enter $D_m$, so $D_m$ increases.

For \emph{MC}, the uncertain part of the dot product is $w_m^T \xm$, whose
variance under $p(\xm \mid \xo)$ is exactly $D_m$.
A larger $D_m$ spreads the distribution of $\sigma(w^T x)$ more widely, pushing
$\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MC}} = \mathrm{Var}[\sigma(w^T x)]$ toward its upper bound of
$1/4$.
\emph{MC variance increases with the missing ratio.}

For \emph{PCPG}, the key observation is what the CF value at a frequency actually
means.
The CF $\phi_X(s) = \E[e^{isX}]$ has modulus at most 1.
Intuitively, $|e^{isX}|=1$ for every realisation, but the complex exponential
$e^{isX}$ rotates in the complex plane as $X$ varies; when $X$ is widely spread,
these rotations point in different directions and cancel when averaged, driving
$|\phi_X(s)|$ toward zero.
Concretely, if $X$ has variance $\sigma^2_X$, then for large $|s|$ the CF decays
at least as fast as $e^{-\sigma^2_X s^2/2}$; a larger variance means faster decay.

In the PCPG integral, the CF is evaluated at the frequency $s = -uw_m$, which
is proportional to the inner sample $u$.
The relevant variance is that of the projection $w_m^T \xm$ onto the classifier
direction, which equals $D_m$.
Hence
\[
  \bigl|\phi_{\xm\mid\xo}(-uw_m)\bigr|
  \;\leq\;
  \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}u^2\,D_m\right).
\]
When $D_m$ is large, this bound drops to nearly zero already for moderate $|u|$
--- the integrand $h(\gamma,u)$ is effectively zero outside a narrow band
$|u| \lesssim 1/\sqrt{D_m}$ around zero.
Larger $D_m$ makes this band narrower: ``stronger CF damping'' means the PCPG
integrand is non-negligible only for very small $u$.

Two consequences follow from a narrow effective integration range.
First, $\sigma^2_A$ decreases: the $e^{1/(8\gamma)}$ blowup that drives inner
variance is large only when $\gamma$ is small, which makes $u \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\gamma)$
small as well.
In this regime the CF is near 1 regardless of $D_m$, so the blowup is not
directly suppressed.
However, for large $\gamma$ --- where most PG mass lies --- the Gaussian
$\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma)$ places $u$ well outside the narrow band, and the CF
kills those samples.
The net effect is fewer extreme inner samples, lowering $\sigma^2_A$.
Second, $\sigma^2_B$ decreases: the inner expected value
$\E_{u\mid\gamma}[Z\mid\gamma]$ is near zero for any $\gamma$ large enough that
$\sqrt{\gamma} \gg 1/\sqrt{D_m}$ (essentially all large PG draws), and near
$1/2$ only for the rare very small $\gamma$ draws.
As $D_m$ grows, the threshold shifts to smaller $\gamma$ and a larger fraction of
PG draws gives $\E[Z|\gamma]\approx 0$, so the variation of this function over
$\gamma$ --- which is $\sigma^2_B$ --- shrinks.
\emph{PCPG variance decreases with the missing ratio.}

The same narrowing of the integrand reduces the Gauss--Hermite quadrature error
for PCPG-GH: a more concentrated integrand is easier to integrate accurately
with a fixed number of nodes, so the bias also decreases with $\rho$.

\paragraph{Effect of total dimension (fixed missing ratio $\rho$).}
As $d$ grows with $\rho$ fixed, $d_m = \rho d$ grows, but so does $d_o = (1-\rho)d$.
Each individual weight scales roughly as $w_j = O(1/\sqrt{d})$ in a well-fit
logistic regression (the discriminative signal is spread over more features), so
each term in $D_m$ is $O(1/d)$.
Summing over $d_m = \rho d$ missing features gives $D_m = O(\rho)$: bounded,
regardless of $d$.

For \emph{MC}, the variance of $w_m^T \xm$ stays at $D_m = O(\rho)$, so the
distribution of $\sigma(w^T x)$ approaches a fixed limit as $d \to \infty$
and $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MC}}$ neither grows nor shrinks.

For \emph{PCPG}, $D_m$ stays bounded, so the CF damping does not improve and
the variances $\sigma^2_A$, $\sigma^2_B$ stay roughly constant.

\emph{Higher total dimension at fixed missing ratio does not improve PCPG over MC.}
The relative advantage of PCPG depends on the missing ratio $\rho$, not on
the total number of features $d$ alone.

\paragraph{Summary.}
As the missing ratio $\rho$ grows: MC variance increases toward $1/(4N)$, while
PCPG variance (both $\sigma^2_A$ and $\sigma^2_B$) decreases due to CF
concentration.
As total dimension $d$ grows at fixed $\rho$: both estimators are largely
unaffected, since $D_m$ stays bounded.
The relative advantage of PCPG over MC is therefore tied to the missing ratio,
and is strongest in the high-missing-data regime where accurate marginalisation
matters most.

\section{Effect of the Classifier's Weight Norm}
\label{sec:weight-norm}

The argument of Section~\ref{sec:dimensionality} for a growing total dimension $d$
assumes a well-fitted classifier, whose individual weights shrink like
$O(1/\sqrt{d})$, so that $z = w^T x$ stays on the same scale. In this section we
show experimentally that this assumption matters. With the default
regularisation of the logistic regression, the norm of $w$ grows with $d$, and
PCPG with a fixed number of nodes loses accuracy at high dimensions. When the
norm of $w$ is held fixed, the effect disappears.

\paragraph{Setup.}
The data are generated with scikit-learn's \texttt{make\_classification}:
10{,}000 samples, of which 2 features are informative, 2 are linear combinations of
these, and all others are independent noise; 8{,}000 samples are used for
training and 2{,}000 for testing. The density $p(x)$ is a GMM with $K = 4$
diagonal components, the classifier a logistic regression without intercept. The
number of features is $d \in \{5, 16, 50, 158, 500, 1581, 5000\}$, a factor
$\sqrt{10}$ apart. For every test point a fraction $\rho \in \{0.3, 0.5, 0.8\}$
of the features is hidden at random. MC and PCPG-Quad get the same budget of
$1{,}216$ evaluations: MC draws $1{,}216$ samples, and PCPG-Quad uses $M = 19$
Golub--Welsch nodes and $n_H = 64$ Gauss--Hermite nodes. The error is the RMSE over 50 test
points against the exact value, which we compute from the Gaussian mixture of
$z \mid \xo$ (Section~\ref{subsec:gmm-direct}) with adaptive quadrature. The time
is that of one batched call over all 2{,}000 test points on a GPU.

\paragraph{Observation.}
Figure~\ref{fig:comparison-default} shows the result with scikit-learn's default
regularisation ($C = 1$). Up to $d = 158$, PCPG-Quad reaches an RMSE between
$2 \cdot 10^{-7}$ and $2 \cdot 10^{-5}$, two to four orders of magnitude below MC,
whose error stays between $3 \cdot 10^{-3}$ and $1.3 \cdot 10^{-2}$ at every $d$.
From $d = 500$ on, the error of PCPG-Quad rises: to $1.2 \cdot 10^{-3}$ and
$1.7 \cdot 10^{-3}$ at 30\% and 50\% missing for $d = 500$, to between
$4 \cdot 10^{-3}$ and $1.1 \cdot 10^{-2}$ for $d = 1581$, and to between
$2 \cdot 10^{-2}$ and $4 \cdot 10^{-2}$ for $d = 5000$, where it is worse than MC.
The errors of MC and of mean imputation hardly depend on $d$.

\paragraph{Cause: the classifier overfits.}
Only four features carry information about the label, so every additional
feature is noise. With $C = 1$ the logistic regression fits this noise once there
are many features compared to the 8{,}000 training points
(Table~\ref{tab:classifier-norm}). The norm of $w$ is about $2.2$ up to $d = 158$
and grows to $2.7$, $6.6$ and $9.5$ at $d = 500$, $1581$ and $5000$. At
$d = 5000$ the classifier reaches 100\% training accuracy, but only 80.5\% test
accuracy, with a test log loss of $1.22$. A larger $w$ makes $z = w^T x$ larger. At
$d = 5000$, the median of $|m_k|$ over all test points and components is between
$8.6$ and $14.9$ (depending on the missing rate), and the median standard
deviation $\sqrt{v_k}$ between $2.8$ and $4.8$. At $d = 16$ they are between $2.4$
and $2.7$, and between $0.5$ and $1.2$.

\paragraph{Why a larger $z$ is harder for PCPG.}
Consider one component of the mixture, $z \mid k \sim \mathcal{N}(m_k, v_k)$, and
substitute $u = \sqrt{2\gamma}\,t$ as in Section~\ref{subsec:gauss-hermite}. The
inner integrand of this component becomes
\[
  e^{1/(8\gamma)}\, e^{\,i a t - b t^2},
  \qquad
  a = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\gamma}} - \sqrt{2\gamma}\, m_k,
  \qquad
  b = \gamma\, v_k,
\]
and Gauss--Hermite has to approximate
\[
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-t^2}\, e^{\,i a t - b t^2}\, \mathrm{d}t
  = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{1 + b}}\, \exp\!\left( -\frac{a^2}{4(1 + b)} \right).
\]
For large $|a|$ this value is exponentially small, but every term of the
quadrature sum has a modulus of up to $\omega_i$. The sum therefore has to cancel
almost completely, which a fixed rule can only do if its nodes resolve the
oscillation $e^{iat}$. The $n_H$ nodes lie within roughly $\pm\sqrt{2 n_H}$ with
a spacing of about $\pi/\sqrt{2 n_H}$, which gives the rule of thumb
$n_H \gtrsim a^2/2$. Whatever error remains is then multiplied by
$e^{1/(8\gamma)}$.

Because of the flip (Section~\ref{subsec:sign-flip}), $m_k$ is mostly negative,
so $a = 1/\sqrt{2\gamma} + \sqrt{2\gamma}\,|m_k|$ grows linearly in $|m_k|$, and
thereby in $\|w\|$. The 19 Golub--Welsch nodes reach up to $\gamma \approx 12.8$.
There, $|m_k| = 2.4$ gives $a \approx 12$, which 64 nodes still roughly resolve,
while $|m_k| = 10$ gives $a \approx 50$, far more than 64 nodes can handle. The
largest nodes carry small weights, so the rule of thumb is only a rough guide,
but it explains the trend. The second parameter $b$ grows with $\|w\|^2$ and makes
the integrand narrower, which also needs more nodes.

Two measurements support this. At $d = 500$ and 30\% missing, doubling the inner
nodes to $n_H = 128$ reduces the error from $1.2 \cdot 10^{-3}$ to
$1.5 \cdot 10^{-5}$, so there the inner rule is the limit. At $d = 5000$, neither
$n_H = 256$ nor the maximal $M = 19$ outer nodes bring the error below about
$7 \cdot 10^{-3}$: the scale of $z$ exceeds what both rules cover.

\paragraph{Holding the norm fixed.}
To separate the effect of $d$ from that of $\|w\|$, we rescale the weights of
the default classifier to unit norm, $w \leftarrow w / \|w\|$, for every $d$.
This keeps the direction of $w$, and with it every decision of the classifier,
and only puts $z = w^T x$ on the same scale for every $d$.

With the norm held fixed (Figure~\ref{fig:comparison-fixed-norm}), the error of
PCPG-Quad no longer depends on $d$. It lies between $3.6 \cdot 10^{-8}$ and
$6 \cdot 10^{-8}$ for every $d$ and missing rate, at the precision of the single
precision floating point numbers used in the computation. This includes
$d = 1581$ and $d = 5000$, where the same classifier with its original norm gave
errors of up to $4 \cdot 10^{-2}$. MC stays between $2 \cdot 10^{-3}$ and
$9 \cdot 10^{-3}$. The scale of $z$ thus explains the whole loss of accuracy at
high dimensions. Since the unit norm is smaller than the norm of about $2.2$ that
the default classifier has at low dimensions, PCPG-Quad is also more accurate
here than in Figure~\ref{fig:comparison-default} at small $d$.

Rescaling is a controlled experiment, not a way to fix the classifier, since it
keeps the overfitted direction of $w$. In practice, stronger regularisation keeps
the norm small and also improves the classifier: with $C$ chosen such that
$\|w\| = 2.2$ at $d = 5000$, the test accuracy rises from 80.5\% to 83.6\% and
the test log loss falls from $1.22$ to $0.40$.

\paragraph{Runtime.}
The bottom rows of both figures are the same, since the time does not depend on
$w$. For all methods it grows linearly in $d$. At the same budget, PCPG-Quad
evaluates the characteristic function at $1{,}216$ frequencies per test point and
MC draws $1{,}216$ samples. From $d = 16$ on, PCPG-Quad is about twice as fast as
MC in our implementation, for example about $1.4$\,s against $3.0$\,s at
$d = 5000$, mostly because MC draws all $d$ features of every sample, including
the observed ones.

\paragraph{Summary.}
The accuracy of PCPG-Quad with a fixed number of nodes depends on the scale of
$z = w^T x$, that is on $m_k$ and $v_k$ relative to the quadrature rules, and not
on the number of features as such. The argument of
Section~\ref{sec:dimensionality} holds for a regularised classifier. An overfitted
classifier makes $z$ large, and then more nodes are needed than a rule chosen for
small $z$ provides. In practice, the classifier should be regularised, which also
improves it. Alternatively, the number of inner nodes can be chosen per test point
from the largest $|m_k|$ and $v_k$.

\section{Special Case: Closed Form for GMMs}
\label{sec:gmm-closed-form}

For a fixed $\gamma$, the integral over $u$ together with the expectation over
$\xm$ only undoes the HS step: they compute
\[
  g(\gamma) := \E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)}\!\left[ \exp\!\left( \tfrac{1}{2}z - \tfrac{1}{2}\gamma z^2 \right) \right],
  \qquad
  p(y \mid \xo) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\E_{\gamma \sim \PG(1,0)}\!\left[ g(\gamma) \right].
\]
HS is needed because, for a general density, $g(\gamma)$ cannot be computed
directly. For a GMM with diagonal covariances it can. Within component $k$,
$z = w_o^T \xo + w_m^T \xm$ is a constant plus a weighted sum of independent
Gaussians, so
\[
  z \mid k \sim \mathcal{N}(m_k, v_k), \qquad
  m_k = w_o^T \xo + w_m^T \mu_k^m, \qquad
  v_k = \sum_{d \in \mathrm{mis}} w_d^2\,\sigma_{k,d}^2 .
\]
The expectation of $\exp(\tfrac{1}{2}z - \tfrac{1}{2}\gamma z^2)$ under a Gaussian
is a Gaussian integral (both exponents are quadratic in $z$), and completing the
square in $z$ gives
\[
  g(\gamma) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \tilde{\pi}_k(\xo)\,
  \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \gamma v_k}}
  \exp\!\left( \frac{m_k + \tfrac{1}{4} v_k - \gamma m_k^2}{2\,(1 + \gamma v_k)} \right).
\]
For $v_k = 0$ (nothing missing) this reduces to the PG identity itself, as it
should. Only the outer integral over $\gamma$ remains, and it can be handled with
MC or the Golub--Welsch rule as before. The integrand is real, positive and
bounded, so the small-$\gamma$ problems discussed above do not occur, and each
value of $\gamma$ costs $K$ operations instead of $K \cdot n_H$.

This does not carry over to general probabilistic circuits. A PC with Gaussian
leaves is also a mixture of Gaussians, but with exponentially many components
(induced trees) that cannot be enumerated. The circuit can only compute
expectations of functions that factorise over the features, and $g(\gamma)$ does
not, because of the $\gamma z^2$ term. After the HS step the exponent is linear in
$z$ and factorises, which is exactly why PCPG needs the characteristic function.
For GMMs the closed form is therefore mainly useful as an exact reference: it
isolates the error introduced by the inner quadrature of PCPG.

\section{Related Work}

Here, we just focus on works that also try to calculate 

\[
  \mathbb{E}_{P(\xm \mid \xo)} \left[\sigma(w^T x)\right]
\]

but use a different approach.

\subsection{Naive Conformant Learning (NaCL)}

\citet{khosravi2019expect} first point out that this expectation is hard
to compute in general: even with a naive Bayes feature distribution, it
is NP-hard. They also note that the popular shortcut of mean imputation
only matches the expectation when the classifier is linear \emph{and} the
features are independent --- for the sigmoid it is always biased.

Their fix is to pick a feature distribution on which the expectation
\emph{is} cheap, namely a naive Bayes model over binary features. The
trick is that naive Bayes and logistic regression are dual to each other:
every naive Bayes induces a logistic regression, and many naive Bayes
models induce the same one. They call a naive Bayes \textit{conformant}
with the given LR if both give the same class probabilities on fully
observed inputs, and learn such a distribution via geometric programming
(``naive conformant learning''). Once this naive Bayes is fitted, the
expected prediction is just a marginal inference query and runs in linear
time.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Difference to us:} they keep the feature distribution
simple (naive Bayes on binary inputs) so the expectation is exact and
cheap; we keep the feature distribution rich (a GMM or PC on continuous
inputs) and instead use a stochastic trick to handle the sigmoid. They
also have to refit the feature distribution whenever the classifier
changes, while ours is trained once and reused.

\subsection{Joint Modelling via SAEM}

\citet{jiang2020logreg} assume a joint Gaussian model on the covariates,
$x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$, together with logistic regression on
top. Their main contribution is on the training side: they fit
$(\mu, \Sigma, \beta)$ jointly from training data that may itself contain
missing values, using a stochastic-approximation EM algorithm with
Metropolis--Hastings updates.

For prediction at test time, they simply do plain Monte Carlo: sample
$x^{m,(s)} \sim p(\xm \mid \xo)$ from the fitted Gaussian and average,
\[
  p(y \mid \xo) \approx \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^{S} p\!\left(y \mid \xo, x^{m,(s)}\right)
\]
This is exactly the MC baseline we compare against, just with a single
Gaussian instead of a mixture as the density.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Difference to us:} they sample the missing features and
average the sigmoid; we never sample the missing features, but instead
rewrite the sigmoid so that only the characteristic function of
$p(\xm \mid \xo)$ is needed. Their feature distribution is also limited
to a single Gaussian, while ours can capture multimodal shapes.
