\section{Bias and Variance}
\label{sec:bias-variance}

We compare the bias and variance of MC with PCPG-MC, which samples both expectations, and PCPG, which computes both by quadrature.

\subsection{MC Estimator}

The MC estimator draws $N$ samples ${\xm}^{(i)} \sim p(\xm \mid \xo)$ and returns
\[
  \widehat{p}_{\mathrm{MC}}(N) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \sigma\!\left(w^T x^{(i)}\right).
\]
\paragraph{Bias.}
MC is unbiased for all $N \geq 1$: sampling from $p(\xm \mid \xo)$ and averaging $\sigma(w^T x)$ is a direct Monte Carlo estimate of the target expectation, so $\E[\widehat{p}_{\mathrm{MC}}(N)] = p(y \mid \xo)$ exactly.

\paragraph{Variance.}
The draws are i.i.d., so the variance of their average is the per-sample variance
$\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MC}} := \mathrm{Var}_{p(\xm\mid\xo)}\!\left[\sigma(w^T x)\right]$
divided by $N$. Since $\sigma(\cdot) \in (0,1)$, the per-sample variance is bounded:
for any $[0,1]$-valued random variable $Y$,
\[
  \mathrm{Var}(Y) = \E[Y^2] - \E[Y]^2 \leq \E[Y] - \E[Y]^2 = \E[Y](1-\E[Y]) \leq \tfrac{1}{4},
\]
using $Y^2 \leq Y$ on $[0,1]$ and $p(1-p) \leq 1/4$. Therefore
\[
  \mathrm{Var}\!\left[\widehat{p}_{\mathrm{MC}}(N)\right] = \frac{\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MC}}}{N} \leq \frac{1}{4N}.
\]

\subsection{PCPG-MC Estimator}

PCPG-MC draws $M$ outer samples $\gamma_j \sim \PG(1,0)$ and, for each of them, $L$ inner samples $u_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \gamma_j)$ (Section~\ref{subsec:pcpg-mc}). With $Z := \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re}[f(u, \gamma)]$ for a single sample, it returns
\[
  \widehat{p}_{\mathrm{PCPG\text{-}MC}}(M,L) = \frac{1}{ML}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{L} Z_{ij}.
\]

\paragraph{Bias.}
PCPG-MC is unbiased as well. Both expectations are approximated by ordinary MC, so by linearity of expectation the estimator equals the true double integral, which is $p(y \mid \xo)$ by the PG and Hubbard--Stratonovich identities.

\paragraph{Output range.}
Since $|\phi_{\xm\mid\xo}| \leq 1$, a single sample satisfies $|Z| \leq \tfrac{1}{2}e^{1/(8\gamma)}$.
Because $\gamma \sim \PG(1,0)$ can be arbitrarily small, this bound is unbounded, so individual PCPG samples are not confined to $[0,1]$ and can be negative or exceed $1$, while every MC sample lies in $(0,1)$.

\paragraph{Variance.}
The estimator has two nested levels of randomness. For a fixed $\gamma_j$, the average of its $L$ inner samples has the conditional variance $\sigma^2_{A,\gamma_j}/L$, with $\sigma^2_{A,\gamma} := \mathrm{Var}_{u \mid \gamma}[Z \mid \gamma]$. The law of total variance over both levels and the $M$ independent outer samples give
\[
  \mathrm{Var}\!\left[\widehat{p}_{\mathrm{PCPG\text{-}MC}}(M,L)\right] = \frac{\sigma^2_A}{ML} + \frac{\sigma^2_B}{M},
  \qquad
  \sigma^2_A := \E_\gamma\bigl[\sigma^2_{A,\gamma}\bigr],
  \quad
  \sigma^2_B := \mathrm{Var}_\gamma\!\left[\E_{u \mid \gamma}[Z \mid \gamma]\right].
\]
$\sigma^2_A$ measures how much the inner expectation fluctuates for a typical $\gamma$, and $\sigma^2_B$ how much $\gamma$ itself shifts the result.

\paragraph{The inner variance is infinite.}
The problem comes from completing the square in Trick~2, which introduced the factor $e^{1/(8\gamma)}$. For a fixed $z$, the inner expectation is known exactly from the HS identity,
\[
  \E_{u \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\gamma)}\!\left[ e^{-iu(z - c)} \right] = \exp\!\left( -\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma \left( z - c \right)^2 \right) \approx e^{-1/(8\gamma)} \quad \text{for small } \gamma,
\]
which is tiny. Multiplied by $e^{1/(8\gamma)}$ it gives a value of order one. So the inner average has to produce a tiny number from terms of modulus one that almost cancel, and every bit of noise in that average is amplified by $e^{1/(8\gamma)}$, which is already about $2.7 \cdot 10^{5}$ for $\gamma = 0.01$.
In terms of variance, a single inner sample has the second moment $e^{1/(4\gamma)}$. Near zero the density of $\PG(1,0)$ decays only like $e^{-1/(8\gamma)}$, which is not fast enough to compensate, so averaged over $\gamma$ the second moment is infinite: formally $\sigma^2_A = \infty$.
Small values of $\gamma$ are rare, which is why the estimator still works in practice, but when they occur they produce extreme samples.

\paragraph{The outer variance shrinks with more missing features.}
For a GMM with diagonal covariances, the characteristic function of the missing features satisfies
\[
  \bigl|\phi_{\xm\mid\xo}(-uw_m)\bigr| \leq \exp\!\left(-\frac{u^2}{2}\,D_m\right), \qquad D_m := \min_k \sum_{j \in \mathrm{mis}} w_j^2\,\sigma_{kj}^2,
\]
where $D_m$ is the variance of the missing part of $w^T x$. When $D_m$ is large, the integrand is nearly zero unless $|u|$ is small, so the inner expectation is close to zero for a wide range of $\gamma$ and hardly varies with it: $\sigma^2_B \approx 0$. More missing features increase $D_m$, so PCPG-MC gains relative to MC as the missing rate grows, while MC behaves the opposite way, since more missing features make $w^T x$ more random and increase $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MC}}$.

\subsection{PCPG Estimator}

\paragraph{Variance.}
Replacing both expectations with quadrature (Gauss--PG for the outer, Gauss--Hermite for the inner) makes the estimator fully deterministic:
\[
  \mathrm{Var}\!\left[\widehat{p}_{\mathrm{PCPG}}\right] = 0.
\]
Its output is bounded, since the nodes $\gamma_j$ are fixed positive numbers and the factor $e^{1/(8\gamma_j)}$ is a known finite constant for each of them. The amplification is mild, because the smallest Gauss--PG node is not close to zero ($\gamma_{\min} \approx 0.046$ for 19 nodes, giving $e^{1/(8\gamma_{\min})} \approx 15$).

\paragraph{Bias.}
Both expectations introduce a quadrature error. Gauss--Hermite with $N$ nodes is exact for polynomials in $u$ up to degree $2N - 1$, and for analytic integrands its error decays super-algebraically in $N$. The Gauss--PG rule with $M$ nodes is exact for polynomials in $\gamma$ up to degree $2M-1$. At $M = 19$ and $N \geq 20$, both errors are numerically negligible for moderate $|w^T x|$. For large positive $w^T x$ the outer error is amplified by $e^{z/2}$, which the sign flip (Section~\ref{subsec:sign-flip}) removes; with the flip, the outer rules fitted directly to the sigmoid (Appendix~\ref{subsubsec:sigmoid-fit}) are more accurate than Gauss--PG with fewer nodes. The inner Gauss--Hermite error for large $|w^T x|$ is not affected by the flip.
The infinite inner variance of PCPG-MC does not disappear completely, however; it turns into quadrature error. With $u = \sqrt{2\gamma}\,t$ the inner integrand contains the factor $\exp(-i(z - c)\sqrt{2\gamma}\,t)$, which oscillates with the frequency $|z - c|\sqrt{2\gamma}$ in the Hermite variable $t$. This frequency is large when $\gamma$ is small, but also when $|w^T x|$ is large. A fixed number of nodes cannot resolve fast oscillations, and the exact inner value it should produce is then tiny, so the rule has to sum terms of modulus one to almost zero, and any error in that sum is multiplied by $e^{1/(8\gamma)}$. The damping of the characteristic function helps when many features are missing, because it suppresses large $|u|$; with few missing features and a confident prediction, the inner quadrature error can become significant.

\paragraph{Bias.}
MC and PCPG-MC are exactly unbiased. PCPG introduces a quadrature error, which
decays fast in the number of nodes and is negligible at the parameter choices
used ($N \geq 20$ Gauss--Hermite and $M = 19$ Gauss--PG nodes), as long as
$|w^T x|$ is moderate.

\paragraph{Variance.}
MC has the variance $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MC}}/N$, bounded by $1/(4N)$. PCPG-MC is
unbiased as well, but its inner variance $\sigma^2_A$ is formally infinite because
of the factor $e^{1/(8\gamma)}$, so rare small values of $\gamma$ produce extreme
samples, and its outer variance $\sigma^2_B$ only becomes small when many features
are missing. PCPG has no variance at all: its error is pure bias from the
quadrature.

\paragraph{When to use which.}
MC is the simplest choice when sampling from $p(\xm \mid \xo)$ is easy. PCPG-MC
mainly serves as a reference: it shows that the identities behind PCPG are exact,
but sampling the two expectations is not competitive. PCPG is the method we
propose: it only needs the characteristic function, and it is deterministic, so
repeated evaluations at the same point agree exactly and it can be differentiated,
for example when training through it.

\section{Effect of Dimensionality}
\label{sec:dimensionality}

Two distinct quantities can vary: the number of missing features $d_m$ at fixed
total dimension $d$ (equivalently, the missing ratio $\rho = d_m/d$), and the
total dimension $d$ itself at a fixed ratio $\rho$.
Their effects are different and we treat them separately.
The key quantity that links both estimators is the \emph{missing-feature energy}
\[
  D_m := \sum_{j \in \mathrm{mis}} w_j^2\,\sigma_{kj}^2,
\]
which measures how much variance the uncertain part of $w^T x$ contributes.

\paragraph{Effect of the missing ratio (more missing features, fixed $d$).}
As $d_m$ grows at fixed $d$, more terms enter $D_m$, so $D_m$ increases.

For \emph{MC}, the uncertain part of the dot product is $w_m^T \xm$, whose
variance under $p(\xm \mid \xo)$ is exactly $D_m$.
A larger $D_m$ spreads the distribution of $\sigma(w^T x)$ more widely, pushing
$\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MC}} = \mathrm{Var}[\sigma(w^T x)]$ toward its upper bound of
$1/4$.
\emph{MC variance increases with the missing ratio.}

For \emph{PCPG}, the key observation is what the CF value at a frequency actually
means.
The CF $\phi_X(s) = \E[e^{isX}]$ has modulus at most 1.
Intuitively, $|e^{isX}|=1$ for every realisation, but the complex exponential
$e^{isX}$ rotates in the complex plane as $X$ varies; when $X$ is widely spread,
these rotations point in different directions and cancel when averaged, driving
$|\phi_X(s)|$ toward zero.
Concretely, if $X$ has variance $\sigma^2_X$, then for large $|s|$ the CF decays
at least as fast as $e^{-\sigma^2_X s^2/2}$; a larger variance means faster decay.

In the PCPG integral, the CF is evaluated at the frequency $s = -uw_m$, which
is proportional to the inner sample $u$.
The relevant variance is that of the projection $w_m^T \xm$ onto the classifier
direction, which equals $D_m$.
Hence
\[
  \bigl|\phi_{\xm\mid\xo}(-uw_m)\bigr|
  \;\leq\;
  \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}u^2\,D_m\right).
\]
When $D_m$ is large, this bound drops to nearly zero already for moderate $|u|$
--- the integrand $h(\gamma,u)$ is effectively zero outside a narrow band
$|u| \lesssim 1/\sqrt{D_m}$ around zero.
Larger $D_m$ makes this band narrower: ``stronger CF damping'' means the PCPG
integrand is non-negligible only for very small $u$.

Two consequences follow from a narrow effective integration range.
First, $\sigma^2_A$ decreases: the $e^{1/(8\gamma)}$ blowup that drives inner
variance is large only when $\gamma$ is small, which makes $u \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\gamma)$
small as well.
In this regime the CF is near 1 regardless of $D_m$, so the blowup is not
directly suppressed.
However, for large $\gamma$ --- where most PG mass lies --- the Gaussian
$\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma)$ places $u$ well outside the narrow band, and the CF
kills those samples.
The net effect is fewer extreme inner samples, lowering $\sigma^2_A$.
Second, $\sigma^2_B$ decreases: the inner expected value
$\E_{u\mid\gamma}[Z\mid\gamma]$ is near zero for any $\gamma$ large enough that
$\sqrt{\gamma} \gg 1/\sqrt{D_m}$ (essentially all large PG draws), and near
$1/2$ only for the rare very small $\gamma$ draws.
As $D_m$ grows, the threshold shifts to smaller $\gamma$ and a larger fraction of
PG draws gives $\E[Z|\gamma]\approx 0$, so the variation of this function over
$\gamma$ --- which is $\sigma^2_B$ --- shrinks.
\emph{The variance of PCPG-MC decreases with the missing ratio.}

The same narrowing of the integrand reduces the Gauss--Hermite quadrature error
for PCPG: a more concentrated integrand is easier to integrate accurately
with a fixed number of nodes, so the bias also decreases with $\rho$.

\paragraph{Effect of total dimension (fixed missing ratio $\rho$).}
As $d$ grows with $\rho$ fixed, $d_m = \rho d$ grows, but so does $d_o = (1-\rho)d$.
Each individual weight scales roughly as $w_j = O(1/\sqrt{d})$ in a well-fit
logistic regression (the discriminative signal is spread over more features), so
each term in $D_m$ is $O(1/d)$.
Summing over $d_m = \rho d$ missing features gives $D_m = O(\rho)$: bounded,
regardless of $d$.

For \emph{MC}, the variance of $w_m^T \xm$ stays at $D_m = O(\rho)$, so the
distribution of $\sigma(w^T x)$ approaches a fixed limit as $d \to \infty$
and $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MC}}$ neither grows nor shrinks.

For \emph{PCPG}, $D_m$ stays bounded, so the CF damping does not improve and
the variances $\sigma^2_A$, $\sigma^2_B$ stay roughly constant.

\emph{Higher total dimension at fixed missing ratio does not improve PCPG over MC.}
The relative advantage of PCPG depends on the missing ratio $\rho$, not on
the total number of features $d$ alone.

\paragraph{Summary.}
As the missing ratio $\rho$ grows: MC variance increases toward $1/(4N)$, while
PCPG variance (both $\sigma^2_A$ and $\sigma^2_B$) decreases due to CF
concentration.
As total dimension $d$ grows at fixed $\rho$: both estimators are largely
unaffected, since $D_m$ stays bounded.
The relative advantage of PCPG over MC is therefore tied to the missing ratio,
and is strongest in the high-missing-data regime where accurate marginalisation
matters most.

\section{Effect of the Classifier's Weight Norm}
\label{sec:weight-norm}

The argument of Section~\ref{sec:dimensionality} for a growing total dimension $d$
assumes a well-fitted classifier, whose individual weights shrink like
$O(1/\sqrt{d})$, so that $z = w^T x$ stays on the same scale. The experiments of
Section~\ref{sec:results-estimate} show that this assumption matters: with the
default regularisation, the norm of $w$ grows with $d$, and PCPG with a fixed
number of nodes loses accuracy at high dimensions. When the norm of $w$ is held
fixed, the effect disappears.

\paragraph{Cause: the classifier overfits.}
Only four features carry information about the label, so every additional
feature is noise. With $C = 1$ the logistic regression fits this noise once there
are many features compared to the 8{,}000 training points
(Table~\ref{tab:classifier-norm}). The norm of $w$ grows from about $2.2$ up to
$d = 158$ to $2.7$, $6.6$ and $9.5$ at $d = 500$, $1581$ and $5000$. At
$d = 5000$ the classifier reaches 100\% training accuracy, but only 80.5\% test
accuracy, with a test log loss of $1.22$. A larger $w$ makes $z = w^T x$ larger. At
$d = 5000$, the median of $|m_k|$ over all test points and components is between
$8.6$ and $14.9$ (depending on the missing rate), and the median standard
deviation $\sqrt{v_k}$ between $2.8$ and $4.8$. At $d = 16$ they are between $2.4$
and $2.7$, and between $0.5$ and $1.2$.

\paragraph{Why a larger $z$ is harder for PCPG.}
Consider one component of the mixture, $z \mid k \sim \mathcal{N}(m_k, v_k)$, and
substitute $u = \sqrt{2\gamma}\,t$ as in Section~\ref{subsec:gauss-hermite}. The
inner integrand of this component becomes
\[
  e^{1/(8\gamma)}\, e^{\,i a t - b t^2},
  \qquad
  a = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\gamma}} - \sqrt{2\gamma}\, m_k,
  \qquad
  b = \gamma\, v_k,
\]
and Gauss--Hermite has to approximate
\[
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-t^2}\, e^{\,i a t - b t^2}\, \mathrm{d}t
  = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{1 + b}}\, \exp\!\left( -\frac{a^2}{4(1 + b)} \right).
\]
For large $|a|$ this value is exponentially small, but every term of the
quadrature sum has a modulus of up to $\omega_i$. The sum therefore has to cancel
almost completely, which a fixed rule can only do if its nodes resolve the
oscillation $e^{iat}$. The $n_H$ nodes lie within roughly $\pm\sqrt{2 n_H}$ with
a spacing of about $\pi/\sqrt{2 n_H}$, which gives the rule of thumb
$n_H \gtrsim a^2/2$. Whatever error remains is then multiplied by
$e^{1/(8\gamma)}$.

Because of the flip (Section~\ref{subsec:sign-flip}), $m_k$ is mostly negative,
so $a = 1/\sqrt{2\gamma} + \sqrt{2\gamma}\,|m_k|$ grows linearly in $|m_k|$, and
thereby in $\|w\|$. The 19 Gauss--PG nodes reach up to $\gamma \approx 12.8$.
There, $|m_k| = 2.4$ gives $a \approx 12$, which 64 nodes still roughly resolve,
while $|m_k| = 10$ gives $a \approx 50$, far more than 64 nodes can handle. The
largest nodes carry small weights, so the rule of thumb is only a rough guide,
but it explains the trend. The second parameter $b$ grows with $\|w\|^2$ and makes
the integrand narrower, which also needs more nodes.

Two measurements support this. At $d = 500$ and 30\% missing, doubling the inner
nodes to $n_H = 128$ reduces the error from $1.2 \cdot 10^{-3}$ to
$1.5 \cdot 10^{-5}$, so there the inner rule is the limit. At $d = 5000$, neither
$n_H = 256$ nor $M = 19$ outer nodes bring the error below about
$7 \cdot 10^{-3}$: the scale of $z$ exceeds what both rules cover.

\paragraph{Holding the norm fixed.}
With the norm held fixed (Figure~\ref{fig:comparison-fixed-norm}), the error of
PCPG is at the precision of the single precision floating point numbers used in
the computation, for every $d$. The scale of $z$ thus explains the whole loss of
accuracy at high dimensions. Since the unit norm is smaller than the norm of
about $2.2$ that the default classifier has at low dimensions, PCPG is also more
accurate there than with the default classifier at small $d$.
Rescaling is a controlled experiment, not a way to fix the classifier, since it
keeps the overfitted direction of $w$. In practice, stronger regularisation keeps
the norm small and also improves the classifier: with $C$ chosen such that
$\|w\| = 2.2$ at $d = 5000$, the test accuracy rises from 80.5\% to 83.6\% and
the test log loss falls from $1.22$ to $0.40$.

\paragraph{Runtime.}
At the same budget, PCPG evaluates the characteristic function at $1{,}216$
frequencies per test point and MC draws $1{,}216$ samples. PCPG is faster in our
implementation mostly because MC draws all $d$ features of every sample,
including the observed ones.

\paragraph{Summary.}
The accuracy of PCPG with a fixed number of nodes depends on the scale of
$z = w^T x$, that is on $m_k$ and $v_k$ relative to the quadrature rules, and not
on the number of features as such. The argument of
Section~\ref{sec:dimensionality} holds for a regularised classifier. An overfitted
classifier makes $z$ large, and then more nodes are needed than a rule chosen for
small $z$ provides. In practice, the classifier should be regularised, which also
improves it. Alternatively, the number of inner nodes can be chosen per test point
from the largest $|m_k|$ and $v_k$.

\section{Training with Missing Features}
\label{sec:disc-training}

This section explains the training results of Section~\ref{sec:training}.

\subsection{Monte Carlo Inside the Loss}
\label{subsec:training-mc}

The obvious alternative to PCPG is to estimate the expectation in the loss by sampling,
\[
  \hat p = \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^{S} \sigma\!\left(w^T x^{(s)}\right),
  \qquad x^{m,(s)} \sim p(\xm \mid \xo),
\]
with new samples in every step. The samples do not depend on $w$, so the
gradient is simply the average of the gradients at the samples. The estimate
$\hat p$ itself is unbiased, but the loss is not: since $\log$ is concave,
Jensen's inequality gives
\[
  \E\left[ -\log \hat p \right] \geq -\log \E[\hat p] = -\log p ,
\]
so the Monte Carlo loss is too large on average. A second-order expansion of
$\log$ around $p$ gives the size of this bias,
\[
  \E\left[ -\log \hat p \right] + \log p \approx
  \frac{\mathrm{Var}[\hat p]}{2 p^2} = \frac{\mathrm{Var}[\sigma(z)]}{2 S p^2},
\]
where $\mathrm{Var}[\sigma(z)]$ is the variance of the prediction over the missing
features. The bias decreases like $1/S$, but it does not average out over the
steps of the optimisation. It acts as an additional penalty on how much the
prediction depends on the missing features, and it is minimised by making $w$
smaller. The Monte Carlo objective therefore has its optimum at a smaller $w$
than the marginal likelihood. For $S = 1$ the loss is the average loss over a
single random completion of every point, $\E[-\log \sigma(\pm w^T x)]$, which is
the objective of training on randomly imputed data (multiple imputation). With
growing $S$, the Monte Carlo objective moves from this objective to the
marginal likelihood.

\subsection{Interpretation of the Results}

\paragraph{The marginal likelihood is reached.}
The baselines minimise a different objective, so their gap does not shrink with
more training: it is the price of ignoring the uncertainty of the missing
features. PCPG follows Exact closely, i.e.\ its quadrature error does not affect
the training. MC lies slightly above it: with $S = 320$ samples its bias
$\mathrm{Var}[\sigma(z)] / (2 S p^2)$ is small, but visible. At both ends of the
range of $d$ the gaps are larger for all three methods including Exact, so the
cause is the optimiser and not the expectation: Adam with a fixed learning rate
does not settle exactly at the optimum. At 5 features it has to reach large
weights ($\lVert w^* \rVert \approx 3$), and Exact stops $7.8 \cdot 10^{-4}$ above
the optimum at 30\% missing, more than the complete-data classifier
($1.1 \cdot 10^{-4}$). At 100 features there are many weights, and all three stop
at about $6 \cdot 10^{-4}$ for 30\% and 50\% missing. PCPG falls behind Exact
with 5 features and 30\% missing because only a single feature is hidden there,
so $z \mid \xo$ is concentrated and the characteristic function hardly decays,
which is the hardest case for the fixed nodes of PCPG.

\paragraph{Accuracy.}
On incomplete test points, the complete-data classifier is optimal for complete
points, not for the expected prediction it is used with, while the marginal
likelihood trains exactly for that prediction. This is why the marginal
likelihood models are more accurate at 80\% missing. On complete test points,
training on incomplete data necessarily loses some of the classifier, and the
loss grows with the missing rate.

\paragraph{Adam against an exact solver.}
At 5 features, Adam does not reach the optimum of the logistic regression. The
objective gaps of the Adam and scikit-learn fits then differ in both directions,
since the gap is measured on the marginal likelihood and not on the objective the
two fits minimise. This is the same limitation of the optimiser as for Exact, and
it does not change the comparison between the models.

\paragraph{Summary.}
PCPG makes the marginal likelihood usable as a training loss: the training with
PCPG reaches the same classifier as training with the exact expectation, without
sampling and without the systematic bias of Monte Carlo. Training on the
marginal likelihood makes the classifier better suited to incomplete test
points than training on complete data, with gains of up to two percentage points
of accuracy at 80\% missing features. On complete test points, it loses less
than mean imputation at high missing rates and moderate dimensions. In terms of
accuracy, the differences between the methods are small on this data, while in
terms of the objective they are clear: only the marginal likelihood models
reach its optimum.

\section{Special Case: Closed Form for GMMs}
\label{sec:gmm-closed-form}

For a fixed $\gamma$, the integral over $u$ together with the expectation over
$\xm$ only undoes the HS step: they compute
\[
  g(\gamma) := \E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)}\!\left[ \exp\!\left( \tfrac{1}{2}z - \tfrac{1}{2}\gamma z^2 \right) \right],
  \qquad
  p(y \mid \xo) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\E_{\gamma \sim \PG(1,0)}\!\left[ g(\gamma) \right].
\]
HS is needed because, for a general density, $g(\gamma)$ cannot be computed
directly. For a GMM with diagonal covariances it can. Within component $k$,
$z = w_o^T \xo + w_m^T \xm$ is a constant plus a weighted sum of independent
Gaussians, so
\[
  z \mid k \sim \mathcal{N}(m_k, v_k), \qquad
  m_k = w_o^T \xo + w_m^T \mu_k^m, \qquad
  v_k = \sum_{d \in \mathrm{mis}} w_d^2\,\sigma_{k,d}^2 .
\]
The expectation of $\exp(\tfrac{1}{2}z - \tfrac{1}{2}\gamma z^2)$ under a Gaussian
is a Gaussian integral (both exponents are quadratic in $z$), and completing the
square in $z$ gives
\[
  g(\gamma) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \tilde{\pi}_k(\xo)\,
  \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \gamma v_k}}
  \exp\!\left( \frac{m_k + \tfrac{1}{4} v_k - \gamma m_k^2}{2\,(1 + \gamma v_k)} \right).
\]
For $v_k = 0$ (nothing missing) this reduces to the PG identity itself, as it
should. Only the outer integral over $\gamma$ remains, and it can be handled with
MC or the Gauss--PG rule as before. The integrand is real, positive and
bounded, so the small-$\gamma$ problems discussed above do not occur, and each
value of $\gamma$ costs $K$ operations instead of $K \cdot n_H$.

This does not carry over to general probabilistic circuits. A PC with Gaussian
leaves is also a mixture of Gaussians, but with exponentially many components
(induced trees) that cannot be enumerated. The circuit can only compute
expectations of functions that factorise over the features, and $g(\gamma)$ does
not, because of the $\gamma z^2$ term. After the HS step the exponent is linear in
$z$ and factorises, which is exactly why PCPG needs the characteristic function.
For GMMs the closed form is therefore mainly useful as an exact reference: it
isolates the error introduced by the inner quadrature of PCPG.


\section{Related Work}

Here, we just focus on works that also try to calculate 

\[
  \mathbb{E}_{P(\xm \mid \xo)} \left[\sigma(w^T x)\right]
\]

but use a different approach.

\subsection{Naive Conformant Learning (NaCL)}

\citet{khosravi2019expect} first point out that this expectation is hard
to compute in general: even with a naive Bayes feature distribution, it
is NP-hard. They also note that the popular shortcut of mean imputation
only matches the expectation when the classifier is linear \emph{and} the
features are independent --- for the sigmoid it is always biased.

Their fix is to pick a feature distribution on which the expectation
\emph{is} cheap, namely a naive Bayes model over binary features. The
trick is that naive Bayes and logistic regression are dual to each other:
every naive Bayes induces a logistic regression, and many naive Bayes
models induce the same one. They call a naive Bayes \textit{conformant}
with the given LR if both give the same class probabilities on fully
observed inputs, and learn such a distribution via geometric programming
(``naive conformant learning''). Once this naive Bayes is fitted, the
expected prediction is just a marginal inference query and runs in linear
time.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Difference to us:} they keep the feature distribution
simple (naive Bayes on binary inputs) so the expectation is exact and
cheap; we keep the feature distribution rich (a GMM or PC on continuous
inputs) and instead use a stochastic trick to handle the sigmoid. They
also have to refit the feature distribution whenever the classifier
changes, while ours is trained once and reused.

\subsection{Joint Modelling via SAEM}

\citet{jiang2020logreg} assume a joint Gaussian model on the covariates,
$x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$, together with logistic regression on
top. Their main contribution is on the training side: they fit
$(\mu, \Sigma, \beta)$ jointly from training data that may itself contain
missing values, using a stochastic-approximation EM algorithm with
Metropolis--Hastings updates.

For prediction at test time, they simply do plain Monte Carlo: sample
$x^{m,(s)} \sim p(\xm \mid \xo)$ from the fitted Gaussian and average,
\[
  p(y \mid \xo) \approx \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^{S} p\!\left(y \mid \xo, x^{m,(s)}\right)
\]
This is exactly the MC baseline we compare against, just with a single
Gaussian instead of a mixture as the density.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Difference to us:} they sample the missing features and
average the sigmoid; we never sample the missing features, but instead
rewrite the sigmoid so that only the characteristic function of
$p(\xm \mid \xo)$ is needed. Their feature distribution is also limited
to a single Gaussian, while ours can capture multimodal shapes.
