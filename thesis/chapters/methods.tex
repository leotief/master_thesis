\section{Core Idea}

\newcommand{\E}{\mathbb{E}}

\subsection{Setup} Consider a logistic regression, with missing features in the observation.\\
Let $x := (\xo, \xm) \in \R^{d}$. Then the logistic regression is defined as
\[
  p(y \mid x) := \sigma (w^T x), \qquad w \in \R^{d}.
\]
Additionally we have a PC that models the density $p(x)$.

\subsection{Goal} Compute the prediction in a probabilistically principled manner using
\[
  p(y \mid \xo) := \E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)} \left[\sigma \left( w^T x \right) \right].
\]

If it were not for the non-linear sigmoid function $\sigma$, we would be able to calculate this in a smooth
and decomposable PC, by pulling the Expectation -which is linear- into the leaf nodes.

Our approach rests on two observations. First, $\sigma(w^T x)$ depends on $x$ only through the single
number $z := w^T x$, so all we need is the distribution of $z$ given $\xo$. Second, this distribution
itself is hard to get from a PC, but its characteristic function is easy. A classical result from
probability theory, the Gil-Pelaez inversion formula \cite{gilpelaez1951}, then gives the expected prediction from the
characteristic function alone.

\subsection{The Characteristic Function of $z$}
\label{subsec:cf-z}

The characteristic function of a random variable $X$ is
$\phi_X(s) := \E \left[ \exp(i s^T X) \right]$.
For $z$ given $\xo$, the expectation is over $\xm$, with $\xo$ fixed, so we split $z = w^T x$ into its observed and missing parts:
\[
  z = w^T x = w_o^T \xo + w_m^T \xm.
\]

The $\xo$ part is constant under $\E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)}$ and factors out:
\begin{align*}
  \phi_z(s) := \E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)} \left[ \exp(isz) \right]
  &= \E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)} \left[ \exp\left(is \left(w_o^T \xo + w_m^T \xm\right)\right) \right] \\
  &= \exp\left(is\, w_o^T \xo\right) \cdot \E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)} \left[ \exp\left(i (s w_m)^T \xm\right) \right]
\end{align*}

This remaining expectation is the characteristic function of the missing features given the observed ones,
evaluated at $s\, w_m$. We get:
\[
  \phi_z(s) = \exp\left(is\, w_o^T \xo\right) \cdot \phi_{\xm \mid \xo}(s\, w_m).
\]

So the observed features only contribute a phase. The function inside the remaining expectation,
$\exp(i s\, w_m^T \xm) = \prod_{d} \exp(i s\, w_d x_d)$, splits into one factor per feature. Unlike the sigmoid,
its expectation can therefore be pushed into the leaves of a smooth and decomposable PC
(Section~\ref{subsec:pc-cf}).

Intuitively, the characteristic function gives the expectation of a wave under the distribution $p(z \mid \xo)$ of $z = w^T x$:
the real part of $\exp(isz)$ is the wave $\cos(sz)$ and the imaginary part the wave $\sin(sz)$, so for every frequency $s$,
$\mathrm{Im}\, \phi_z(s) = \E[\sin(sz) \mid \xo]$ is the signed area under the wave $\sin(sz)$ times $p(z \mid \xo)$
(see Appendix~\ref{app:cf-area} for the derivation). Figure~\ref{fig:cfi-cf} shows this for two different waves, i.e.\ different values of $s$.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/cfi/cf.pdf}
  \caption[The characteristic function]{The characteristic function graphically.
    The expectation of a wave under $p(z)$ is the signed area under the wave times $p(z)$ (shaded). Fast waves average to zero, while slower waves give a non-zero value.}
  \label{fig:cfi-cf}
\end{figure}

Now, we have the characteristic function of $z$, but not yet a way to get the expected prediction from it.

\subsection{The Sigmoid as a Probability}

The sigmoid is the cumulative distribution function of the standard logistic distribution. That means, for
$L \sim \mathrm{Logistic}(0, 1)$ we have $P(L \leq t) = \sigma(t)$ for every $t \in \R$.

If we let $L$ be independent of $x$, and insert this into our expectation, we get:
\begin{align*}
  p(y \mid \xo)
  &= \E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)} \left[ \sigma(z) \right] \\
  &= \E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)} \left[ P(L \leq z) \right] \\
  &= P(L \leq z \mid \xo) \\
  &= P(z - L \geq 0 \mid \xo),
\end{align*}
where the last two probabilities are over both $z$ and $L$.

Intuitively, this means the logistic regression predicts $y = 1$ when $z$ exceeds a random threshold $L$.
With missing features, $z$ is random as well, and the expected prediction is the probability that the random $z$
exceeds the random threshold, i.e.\ that the random variable $z - L$ is positive.

\subsection{The Characteristic Function of the Difference}

For independent random variables $X$ and $Y$ the characteristic function of their difference is the product
$\phi_{X - Y}(s) = \phi_X(s)\, \phi_Y(-s)$. The characteristic function of the standard logistic distribution is
known in closed form,
\[
  \phi_L(s) = \frac{\pi s}{\sinh(\pi s)},
\]
with $\phi_L(0) = 1$. It is real and even, so $\phi_L(-s) = \phi_L(s)$, and
\[
  \phi_{z - L}(s) = \phi_z(s)\, \frac{\pi s}{\sinh(\pi s)}.
\]

So the characteristic function of $z - L$ is the one of $z$, which the PC gives us, times a known factor.
What is left is to get the probability that $z - L$ is positive from its characteristic function.

\subsection{Gil-Pelaez Inversion}

To do this we use the Gil-Pelaez inversion formula \cite{gilpelaez1951}. For a continuous random variable $Y$, it states:
\begin{equation}
  P(Y > 0) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^\infty \frac{\mathrm{Im}\, \phi_Y(s)}{s} \, \mathrm{d}s.
\end{equation}

The idea behind it is that the step function, which is $0$ for negative and $1$ for positive values, can be written as a sum of
sine waves, $\mathbf{1}[y > 0] = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^\infty \frac{\sin(sy)}{s} \mathrm{d}s$. Taking the expectation of
every wave gives $\E[\sin(sY)] = \mathrm{Im}\, \phi_Y(s)$, and with that the formula.

Since $z - L$ is continuous, $P(z - L \geq 0) = P(z - L > 0)$. With $Y = z - L$, and since $\frac{\pi s}{\sinh(\pi s)}$
is real, we get:
\begin{align}
  p(y \mid \xo)
  &= \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^\infty \frac{\mathrm{Im}\, \phi_{z - L}(s)}{s} \, \mathrm{d}s \notag \\
  &= \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^\infty \frac{1}{s}\, \mathrm{Im} \left[ \phi_z(s)\, \frac{\pi s}{\sinh(\pi s)} \right] \mathrm{d}s \notag \\
  &= \frac{1}{2} + \int_0^\infty \frac{\mathrm{Im}\, \phi_z(s)}{\sinh(\pi s)} \, \mathrm{d}s.
  \label{eq:gil-pelaez}
\end{align}

This is exact. At $s = 0$ the integrand stays finite: $\mathrm{Im}\, \phi_z(s) \approx s\, \E[z \mid \xo]$ and $\sinh(\pi s) \approx \pi s$
for small $s$, so it tends to $\E[z \mid \xo] / \pi$.

As a check, take a fixed $z$, i.e.\ no missing features. Then $\phi_z(s) = \exp(isz)$ and
$\mathrm{Im}\, \phi_z(s) = \sin(sz)$. With the known integral $\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin(sz)}{\sinh(\pi s)} \mathrm{d}s = \frac{1}{2} \tanh\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)$ we get
\[
  \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} \tanh\left(\frac{z}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} = \sigma(z).
\]

Intuitively, this means the sigmoid is replaced with a lot of sine waves $\sin(sz)$ added together, one for every frequency $s$,
each with the weight $\frac{1}{\sinh(\pi s)}$. Slow waves get a large weight, and fast waves an exponentially small one.
The sigmoid is a smoothed step, and this smoothing is what turns the weight $\frac{1}{\pi s}$ of the step into $\frac{1}{\sinh(\pi s)}$.
Since the expectation is linear, the expected prediction replaces every wave by its expectation under $p(z \mid \xo)$, which is
exactly $\mathrm{Im}\, \phi_z(s)$.
Figure~\ref{fig:cfi-waves} shows how the sum of the slowest waves closes in on the sigmoid.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/cfi/waves.pdf}
  \caption[The sigmoid as a sum of sine waves]{The sigmoid as a sum of sine waves.
    The sums of the slowest waves $\sin(sz)$, each weighted by $\frac{1}{\sinh(\pi s)}$, at the frequencies of the trapezoid rule (Section~\ref{sec:cfi-integral}), close in on $\sigma(z)$.}
  \label{fig:cfi-waves}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Full Formula}

Plugging the characteristic function of $z$ from Section~\ref{subsec:cf-z} into Equation~\eqref{eq:gil-pelaez}, we obtain:
\[
  p(y \mid \xo) = \frac{1}{2} + \int_0^\infty \frac{1}{\sinh(\pi s)}\, \mathrm{Im} \left[ \exp\left(is\, w_o^T \xo\right) \phi_{\xm \mid \xo}(s\, w_m) \right] \mathrm{d}s.
\]

We propose this as a final expression that we can tractably evaluate. We call this method \emph{CFI}, for characteristic-function inversion.

\subsection{Summary}

The expected prediction $p(y \mid \xo)$ is the area under $\sigma(z)\, p(z)$.
We write the sigmoid as the probability that $z$ exceeds a random logistic threshold. This probability we get from the
characteristic function of $z$ with the Gil-Pelaez formula, which adds up sine waves weighted by $\frac{1}{\sinh(\pi s)}$.
The expectation of each wave under $p(z)$ is the characteristic function, which the PC computes.

\section{Calculating the Integral}
\label{sec:cfi-integral}

After the Gil-Pelaez inversion, $p(y \mid \xo)$ is a one-dimensional integral over the frequency $s$. There is no closed form solution
for it, so we need to approximate it. We take a set of points (nodes) and associated weights, and replace the integral with a weighted sum.

We write $g(s)$ for the term inside the integral,
\[
  g(s) := \frac{1}{\sinh(\pi s)}\, \mathrm{Im} \left[ \exp\left(is\, w_o^T \xo\right) \phi_{\xm \mid \xo}(s\, w_m) \right],
\]
with $g(0) = \E[z \mid \xo] / \pi$.

\subsection{Trapezoid Rule}

We use the trapezoid rule with step $h$ and $K$ nodes $s_k = kh$. Every node gets the weight $h$, except the one at $s_0 = 0$, which gets $\frac{h}{2}$.
The resulting estimator is
\[
  p(y \mid \xo) \approx \frac{1}{2} + h \left( \frac{\E[z \mid \xo]}{2\pi} + \sum_{k=1}^{K} g(kh) \right).
\]

The integrand is smooth, and for smooth integrands the trapezoid rule converges exponentially fast in the number of nodes.
Two things have to be chosen: where to stop, and the step $h$.

\subsection{Truncation}

Since $|\phi_z(s)| \leq 1$, the part of the integral beyond $S = Kh$ is bounded by
\[
  \int_S^\infty \frac{1}{\sinh(\pi s)} \, \mathrm{d}s = \frac{1}{\pi} \log \coth\left(\frac{\pi S}{2}\right) \approx \frac{2}{\pi} e^{-\pi S}.
\]
For $S = 12$ this is about $3 \cdot 10^{-17}$, below double precision. So we never need frequencies beyond $S = 12$.

\subsection{Step Size}

The step $h$ is the important choice. For a fixed $z$ the trapezoid sum
$h \sum_{k} \frac{\sin(khz)}{\sinh(\pi k h)}$ is periodic in $z$ with period $\frac{2\pi}{h}$. So it can only
approximate the sigmoid on one period, roughly for $|z| < \frac{\pi}{h}$. Outside of it, the sum repeats instead
of staying close to $0$ or $1$. The expected prediction is therefore accurate as long as $p(z \mid \xo)$ has
nearly all its mass within $|z| < \frac{\pi}{h}$.

We choose $h$ for every point from the mean $\mu_z = \E[z \mid \xo]$ and the standard deviation
$\sigma_z = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[z \mid \xo]}$ of $z$, so that the period covers $\mu_z \pm 6 \sigma_z$ with a margin of $1$ for the width of
the sigmoid itself, and so that the nodes reach $S$:
\[
  h = \min \left( \frac{\pi}{|\mu_z| + 6 \sigma_z + 1}, \frac{S}{K} \right).
\]
If the first term is the smaller one, the nodes stop before $S$. For a wide $p(z \mid \xo)$ this does not matter, since the
characteristic function then decays quickly. For a narrow $p(z \mid \xo)$ far away from zero, $K$ has to be large enough, $K \geq S (|\mu_z| + 6\sigma_z + 1) / \pi$.

The step only affects the error, not the value that is approximated. So it can be chosen for every point separately, and it needs
no gradient in training.

The mean and the variance of $z$ follow from the mean and the covariance of the missing features,
\[
  \mu_z = w_o^T \xo + w_m^T \E[\xm \mid \xo], \qquad \sigma_z^2 = w_m^T\, \mathrm{Cov}[\xm \mid \xo]\, w_m.
\]
For GMMs and PCs both are available tractably (Section~\ref{sec:density-cf}).

\subsection{Cost}

The method needs $K$ evaluations of the characteristic function per point. All of them are at multiples $s_k w_m$ of the same
vector $w_m$, so they can be done in one batched pass through the density model.

\section{The Characteristic Function of the Density Model}
\label{sec:density-cf}

The only places where the density model is used are the conditional
characteristic function $\phi_{\xm \mid \xo}$, and the mean and variance of $z$ for the step size. In this section we describe how to compute them for the two
density models we use, GMMs and PCs.

\subsection{Gaussian Mixture Models}
\label{subsec:gmm-cf}

For a Gaussian $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$, the characteristic function
is known in closed form,
\[
  \phi_X(s) = \E\left[\exp(i s^T X)\right] = \exp \left( i s^T \mu - \tfrac{1}{2} s^T \Sigma s \right).
\]

A GMM has the density $p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_k, \Sigma_k)$.
Since the expectation is linear, the characteristic function of a
mixture is just a mixture of the characteristic functions of its components,
\[
  \phi_X(s) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\, \exp \left( i s^T \mu_k - \tfrac{1}{2} s^T \Sigma_k s \right).
\]

However, we need the conditional characteristic function $\phi_{\xm \mid \xo}$, so the
characteristic function of the missing features given the observed ones. To get it, we
first need the conditional density $p(\xm \mid \xo)$, which with diagonal covariances, is the mixture:
\[
  p(\xm \mid \xo) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \tilde{\pi}_k(\xo)\, \mathcal{N}(\xm; \mu_k^m, \Sigma_k^m),
\]
with the responsibilities
\[
  \tilde{\pi}_k(\xo) = p(k \mid \xo) = \frac{\pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(\xo; \mu_k^o, \Sigma_k^o)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \pi_j\, \mathcal{N}(\xo; \mu_j^o, \Sigma_j^o)}.
\]

Its components are the original ones restricted to the missing features, with
mean $\mu_k^m$ and covariance $\Sigma_k^m$. The responsibilities are the posterior
weights for each component. They state how well each component explains the
observed features.

The conditional characteristic function is then just the characteristic function of the conditional mixture, so
\[
  \phi_{\xm \mid \xo}(s) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \tilde{\pi}_k(\xo) \, \exp \left( i s^T \mu_k^m - \tfrac{1}{2} s^T \Sigma_k^m s \right).
\]

We compute the responsibilities for every point $\xo$ once and reuse them for all frequencies $s$, since
they only depend on the observed features.
This is shown in algorithm~\ref{alg:gmm-cf}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Conditional characteristic function of a GMM}
\label{alg:gmm-cf}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require GMM parameters $\{\pi_k, \mu_k, \Sigma_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ with diagonal $\Sigma_k = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_k^2)$, partial observation $\xo$, frequencies $s_1, \dots, s_L$ over the missing features
\For{$k = 1, \dots, K$}
  \State $\tilde{\pi}_k \gets \dfrac{\pi_k \, \mathcal{N}(\xo;\mu_k^o,\Sigma_k^o)}{\sum_{j}\pi_j \,\mathcal{N}(\xo;\mu_j^o,\Sigma_j^o)}$
\EndFor
\For{$l = 1, \dots, L$}
  \State $\phi_l \gets \sum_{k=1}^{K} \tilde{\pi}_k \exp \left( i s_l^T \mu_k^m - \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{d \in \mathrm{mis}} s_{ld}^2\, \sigma_{kd}^2 \right)$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\phi_1, \dots, \phi_L$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Within component $k$, $z$ is Gaussian with mean $m_k$ and variance $v_k$ (Section~\ref{subsec:gmm-direct}), so the mean and variance of $z$ are
\[
  \mu_z = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \tilde{\pi}_k(\xo)\, m_k, \qquad \sigma_z^2 = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \tilde{\pi}_k(\xo) \left( v_k + (m_k - \mu_z)^2 \right).
\]
The variance is the variance within the components plus the spread of their means.

\subsection{Probabilistic Circuits}
\label{subsec:pc-cf}

For a PC, the characteristic function can be computed in the same way as the marginal likelihood \cite{yu2023characteristic}.
The function inside the expectation splits into one factor per feature, $\exp(i s^T x) = \prod_{d} \exp(i s_d x_d)$.
In a smooth and decomposable circuit, the expectation of such a product can be moved down to the leaves:
a sum node takes the weighted sum of its children, and a product node the product of its children, which
have disjoint scopes. So we set the leaf of every missing feature $d$ to the characteristic function of the leaf at $s_d$,
the leaf of every observed feature to its density at $x_d$, and evaluate the circuit bottom-up. This gives
$\int \exp(i s^T \xm)\, p(\xo, \xm)\, \mathrm{d}\xm = p(\xo)\, \phi_{\xm \mid \xo}(s)$, and dividing by $p(\xo)$ gives $\phi_{\xm \mid \xo}(s)$.
For Gaussian leaves, the leaf value is $\exp(i s_d \mu_d - \frac{1}{2} s_d^2 \sigma_d^2)$, and for categorical leaves it is $\sum_v p(x_d = v)\, e^{i s_d v}$.

The values are complex, and the products over many leaves can underflow, so in practice the circuit is
evaluated in the log domain with a complex logarithm.

The mean and the variance of $z$ are computed with the same kind of pass. Every node $n$ carries three numbers: its value $P_n$ as in the
marginal likelihood, and the first and second moments $M_n = \int z_n\, p_n$ and $Q_n = \int z_n^2\, p_n$ of the part $z_n$ of $z$ over its scope.
A sum node takes the weighted sums of the three numbers of its children. For a product node with children $a$ and $b$, $z_n = z_a + z_b$ with independent parts, so
\[
  P_n = P_a P_b, \qquad M_n = M_a P_b + P_a M_b, \qquad Q_n = Q_a P_b + 2 M_a M_b + P_a Q_b.
\]
An observed leaf $d$ has $P = p(x_d)$, $M = w_d x_d\, p(x_d)$ and $Q = w_d^2 x_d^2\, p(x_d)$, and a missing Gaussian leaf has $P = 1$, $M = w_d \mu_d$ and
$Q = w_d^2 (\sigma_d^2 + \mu_d^2)$. At the root, $\mu_z = M / P$ and $\sigma_z^2 = Q / P - \mu_z^2$.

PC frameworks usually don't provide a method for it. So we need to implement it ourselves.

The framework SPFlow already offers conditional sampling so we would only need to implement the CF and the moments.
The framework cirkit doesn't have either.

\section{Algorithm}
\label{sec:algorithm}

Putting everything together leads to the CFI algorithm shown in Algorithm~\ref{alg:cfi}.
For one test point, it chooses the step from the mean and variance of $z$, evaluates the density's conditional
characteristic function at the $K$ frequencies, and returns the trapezoid sum. The density model is only used
through its characteristic function and the first two moments of $z$, so the algorithm works for every model that can expose them.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{CFI}
\label{alg:cfi}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require LR weights $w$, partial observation $\xo$, density model with conditional characteristic function $\phi_{\xm \mid \xo}$ and the moments $\E[z \mid \xo]$, $\mathrm{Var}[z \mid \xo]$, number of frequencies $K$, $S = 12$
\State Split $w = (w_o, w_m)$ by the observed/missing mask
\State $\mu_z \gets \E[w^T x \mid \xo]$, \quad $\sigma_z \gets \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[w^T x \mid \xo]}$
\State $h \gets \min \left( \pi / (|\mu_z| + 6 \sigma_z + 1),\, S / K \right)$
\State $s_k \gets k h$ for $k = 1, \dots, K$
\State $\phi_k \gets \exp(i s_k\, w_o^T \xo)\, \phi_{\xm \mid \xo}(s_k\, w_m)$ for all $k$
\State $p \gets \dfrac{1}{2} + h \left( \dfrac{\mu_z}{2\pi} + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \dfrac{\mathrm{Im}(\phi_k)}{\sinh(\pi s_k)} \right)$
\State \Return $p$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\section{PCLR: Training the Density and the Classifier Jointly}
\label{sec:pclr}

Up to now, the classifier $w$ was trained on complete data and features were only
missing at test time. However, in practice, training data could also have missing features.

In this section we treat the density model and the logistic regression as one model of the features and the label together, and train both
jointly on the incomplete training data. We call this joint model \emph{PCLR}.

\subsection{The Joint Model}

PCLR models the features with the density $p_\theta(x)$, with parameters $\theta$, and the label given the features with the logistic regression,
\[
  p_{\theta, w}(x, y) = p_\theta(x)\, p_w(y \mid x), \qquad p_w(y = 1 \mid x) = \sigma(w^T x).
\]

For an incomplete training point we only observe $\xo$ and $y$, so its likelihood is the joint density with the missing features integrated out:
\begin{align*}
  p_{\theta, w}(\xo, y)
  &= \int p_\theta(\xo, \xm)\, p_w(y \mid \xo, \xm) \, \mathrm{d}\xm \\
  &= p_\theta(\xo) \int p_\theta(\xm \mid \xo)\, p_w(y \mid \xo, \xm) \, \mathrm{d}\xm \\
  &= p_\theta(\xo)\, \E_{p_\theta(\xm \mid \xo)} \left[ p_w(y \mid x) \right] \\
  &= p_\theta(\xo)\, p_{\theta, w}(y \mid \xo).
\end{align*}

So the likelihood of a point splits into two terms: the marginal likelihood $p_\theta(\xo)$ of the observed features, and
the expected prediction $p_{\theta, w}(y \mid \xo)$ of its label, which is exactly what we compute with Algorithm~\ref{alg:cfi}.
The density appears in both. It does not only have to explain the features, but also the missing features have to be such that the
classifier can predict the label from them.

\subsection{The Objective}

We fit $\theta$ and $w$ together by minimizing the negative log-likelihood over all training points, with a weight $\lambda$ for the label term
and an $L_2$ penalty on $w$:
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta, w) = -\frac{1}{n_\mathrm{train}} \sum_{n=1}^{n_\mathrm{train}} \left( \log p_\theta(\xo_n) + \lambda \log p_{\theta, w}(y_n \mid \xo_n) \right) + \frac{1}{2 C n_\mathrm{train}} \lVert w \rVert^2 .
  \label{eq:pclr}
\end{equation}

For $\lambda = 1$ this is the maximum likelihood estimate of the joint model. The density term sums over all observed features, while the label
term is only one number per point, so with many features the density term dominates. A larger $\lambda$ gives the label more weight, and
trades a little density quality for a better classifier.

The penalty is the $L_2$ penalty of scikit-learn's logistic regression, with $C = 1$.
It keeps $w$ from growing without bound and makes the optimum unique.

\subsection{The Density Term}

The marginal likelihood of the observed features is
\[
  p_\theta(\xo) = \int p_\theta( x = (\xo, \xm)) \, \mathrm{d}\xm.
\]

In general this integral is hard, but can be computed tractably for a GMM or a PC.

For a GMM, we can see this one component $k$ at a time. A component is a
Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\mu_k, \Sigma_k)$, and since our GMM uses diagonal covariances
$\Sigma_k = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{k1}^2, \dots, \sigma_{kD}^2)$,
every feature $d$, within a component, has its own one-dimensional Gaussian with
mean $\mu_{kd}$ and variance $\sigma_{kd}^2$. This means that the features are independent of each other
within a component. Since the density of independent variables is the product of their individual densities,
the density of a point x in component k can be written as
\[
  p(x \mid k) = \prod_{d=1}^{D} \mathcal{N}(x_d; \mu_{kd}, \sigma_{kd}^2).
\]

Now when a feature $d$ is missing, integrating over $x_d$ only touches its own
term $\mathcal{N}(x_d; \mu_{kd}, \sigma_{kd}^2)$.
Because that term is a density, it integrates to one and it can simply be dropped out of the product.
Then only the terms of the observed features remain,
\[
  p(\xo) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \prod_{d \in \mathrm{obs}} \mathcal{N}(x_d; \mu_{kd}, \sigma_{kd}^2).
\]

A PC works the same way. Its leaves are the one-dimensional densities of single
features, and when the circuit is smooth and decomposable, the integral over a
missing feature can be moved down to its leaves. There the
leaves of the missing features are simply set to one and the circuit is evaluated as
usual.

\subsection{The Label Term}

The label term is the expected prediction of the logistic regression,
\[
  p_n = p_{\theta, w}(y_n = 1 \mid \xo_n) = \E_{p_\theta(\xm \mid \xo_n)} \left[ \sigma(w^T x) \right],
\]
which we compute with Algorithm~\ref{alg:cfi}. Here $p_{\theta, w}(y_n \mid \xo_n)$ means the probability of the true label of point $n$: it is
$p_n$ if $y_n = 1$ and $1 - p_n$ if $y_n = 0$:
\[
  \log p_{\theta, w}(y_n \mid \xo_n) = y_n \log p_n + (1 - y_n) \log (1 - p_n).
\]
This is the familiar binary cross-entropy. Since the computed expected predictions can lie slightly outside $[0, 1]$ due to the numerical error,
we clamp them to $[\varepsilon, 1 - \varepsilon]$.

\subsection{Gradients}

Both terms are differentiable in all parameters. The density term only depends on $\theta$. The label term depends on $w$ through
$z = w^T x$, and on $\theta$ through the characteristic function $\phi_{\xm \mid \xo}$ of the density, which is a smooth function of the
means, variances and weights of the GMM or the PC. The step size $h$ needs no gradient (Section~\ref{sec:cfi-integral}).
So automatic differentiation gives the gradients $\nabla_\theta J$ and $\nabla_w J$, and we can use any gradient-based optimizer, like Adam.

This is where the deterministic computation of the expected prediction pays off. With Monte Carlo, the samples of the missing features
would have to be differentiated with respect to $\theta$, which is not possible directly, since the choice of a mixture component is discrete.

\subsection{Fixed Density}
\label{subsec:fixed-density}

A simpler variant fits the density first, on the density term alone, and keeps it fixed. Then only the label term remains to be
minimized over $w$:
\begin{equation}
  J(w) = -\frac{1}{n_\mathrm{train}} \sum_{n=1}^{n_\mathrm{train}} \log p_\theta(y_n \mid \xo_n) + \frac{1}{2 C n_\mathrm{train}} \lVert w \rVert^2 .
  \label{eq:marginal-likelihood}
\end{equation}
This is a standard logistic regression, with the prediction replaced by the expected prediction. It is cheaper, and it is also a good starting point for the joint training.

\subsection{Algorithm}
\label{subsec:training-algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:training} puts everything together. The density is initialized by fitting it on the density term alone, so that the
expected predictions are meaningful from the start, and the classifier starts at $w = 0$. Then both are trained by Adam on minibatches.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Training PCLR}
\label{alg:training}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require incomplete training points $(\xo_n, y_n)_{n=1}^{n_\mathrm{train}}$, number of frequencies $K$, weight $\lambda$, penalty $C$, epochs $E$, batch size $B$, learning rate $\eta$, $\varepsilon$
\State Initialize the density model: $\theta \gets \arg\max_\theta \sum_{n} \log p_\theta(\xo_n)$
\State $w \gets 0$
\For{$e = 1, \dots, E$}
  \For{every minibatch $\mathcal{B}$ of $B$ training points, in random order}
    \For{$n \in \mathcal{B}$}
      \State $\ell_n \gets \log p_\theta(\xo_n)$
      \State $p_n \gets$ the expected prediction of $\xo_n$ with Algorithm~\ref{alg:cfi}, clamped to $[\varepsilon, 1 - \varepsilon]$
    \EndFor
    \State $J \gets -\frac{1}{B} \sum_{n \in \mathcal{B}} \left( \ell_n + \lambda \left( y_n \log p_n + (1 - y_n) \log (1 - p_n) \right) \right) + \frac{1}{2 C n_\mathrm{train}} \lVert w \rVert^2$
    \State $(\theta, w) \gets \mathrm{Adam}\left((\theta, w), (\nabla_\theta J, \nabla_w J), \eta\right)$
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return $\theta$ and $w$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

At test time, PCLR predicts with the same expected prediction, with the trained $\theta$ and $w$. So training and prediction use the same
model, and a missing feature is handled the same way in both.

\section{A Direct Route for Gaussian Mixtures}
\label{subsec:gmm-direct}

Although we use GMMs as density models throughout, for a GMM the expected prediction
can also be computed directly, without the characteristic function. We use it as a reference,
to compare the results of the other methods with.

In this section we show how the expected prediction can be computed directly for a GMM.

\subsection{Reducing the Integral to One Dimension}

The prediction $\sigma(w^T x)$ does't depend on individual features, but only
on the single number
\[
  z = w^T x = w_o^T \xo + w_m^T \xm.
\]

The observed part $w_o^T \xo$ is fixed. Only the missing part is random. So
instead of averaging $\sigma$ over all missing features, we can average it just over
$z$, and that is always a one-dimensional number.
This reduces the dimensionality of the integral from the number of missing
features to one, which makes it much easier to compute.
The only thing we need for this is the distribution $p(z \mid \xo)$.

\subsection{The Distribution of $z$}

For a GMM, the distribution of $z$ is relatively easy to find. 

Let's first rewrite $z$ as,

\[
  z = w_o^T \xo + \sum_{d \in \mathrm{mis}} w_d\, x_d,
\]

where the only uncertain part is the sum of the missing features.

Then recall that every missing feature is a one-dimensional Gaussian of its own, and the missing
features are independent of each other,
\[
  x_d \mid k \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{kd}, \sigma_{kd}^2) \quad \text{for every } d \in \mathrm{mis}.
\]

A weighted sum of independent Gaussians is again Gaussian. So within component $k$, $z$ is Gaussian as well,
$z \mid k \sim \mathcal{N}(m_k, v_k)$, and we only need its mean $m_k$ and its
variance $v_k$.

The mean of a sum is the sum of the means, and the mean of $w_d x_d$ is
$w_d \mu_{kd}$. So the mean of $z$ is $z$ with every missing feature replaced by
its mean,
\[
  m_k = \E[z \mid k] = w_o^T \xo + \sum_{d \in \mathrm{mis}} w_d\, \E[x_d \mid k] = w_o^T \xo + \sum_{d \in \mathrm{mis}} w_d\, \mu_{kd}.
\]

Similarly, the variance of their sum is the sum of their
variances, and multiplying $x_d$ by $w_d$ multiplies its variance by $w_d^2$. So
\[
  v_k = \mathrm{Var}[z \mid k] = \sum_{d \in \mathrm{mis}} \mathrm{Var}[w_d\, x_d \mid k] = \sum_{d \in \mathrm{mis}} w_d^2\, \sigma_{kd}^2.
\]

Lastly, we don't  know which component the point comes from. Every component
gives its own Gaussian for $z$, and the observed features tell us how likely each
component is, through the responsibilities $\tilde{\pi}_k(\xo)$ of
Section~\ref{subsec:gmm-cf}. The distribution of $z$ is therefore the mixture of
these Gaussians, weighted by the responsibilities,
\[
  p(z \mid \xo) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \tilde{\pi}_k(\xo)\, \mathcal{N}(z; m_k, v_k).
\]

\subsection{Computing the Expected Prediction}

The expected prediction then splits into one average per component,
\[
  p(y \mid \xo) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \tilde{\pi}_k(\xo)\, \E_{z \sim \mathcal{N}(m_k, v_k)} \left[ \sigma(z) \right].
\]

Each of these averages is a one-dimensional integral of the sigmoid against a
Gaussian. While it doesn't have a closed form solution, it can be very accurately computed using 
numerical integration, like Gauss-Hermite quadrature (Section~\ref{subsec:bg-gauss-hermite}) or adaptive quadrature using \texttt{scipy.integrate.quad}.

\[
  p(y \mid \xo) \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \tilde{\pi}_k(\xo) \sum_{i=1}^{N} \omega_i\, \sigma\!\left( m_k + \sqrt{2 v_k}\, t_i \right).
\]

\subsection{Why This Does Not Work for General PCs}

The direct route needs two things: that we can go through the components one by
one, and that $z$ is Gaussian within each of them. A PC is also a mixture, but the
number of its components grows exponentially with its size, so going through them
one by one is only possible for small circuits. 

Also a general PC could have non-Gaussian leaves, which would make $z$ not Gaussian within a component.
