\section{Core Idea}

\subsection{Setup} Consider a logistic regression, with missing features in the observation.\\ 
Let $x := (\xo, \xm) \in \R^{d}$. Then the logistic regression is defined as
\[
  p(y \mid x) := \sigma (w^T x), \qquad w \in \R^{d}.
\]
Additionally we have a PC that models the density $p(x)$.

\subsection{Goal} Compute the prediction in a probabilistically principled manner using
\[
  p(y \mid \xo) := \E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)} \left[\sigma \left( w^T x \right) \right].
\]

If it were not for the non-linear sigmoid function $\sigma$, we would be able to calculate this in a smooth 
and decomposable PC, by pulling the Expectation -which is linear- into the leaf nodes. To tackle this we propose to use two tricks.

\subsection{Trick 1: Pólya-Gamma}

To replace the sigmoid function we use the PG-Sigmoid identity, shown in \cite{polyagamma}, that states:

\[
  \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{2} \exp \left(\frac{1}{2}z\right) \
  \E_{\gamma \sim \PG(1,0)} \left[\exp \left(-\frac{1}{2} \gamma z^2 \right)\right]
\]
for $z \in \R$.

Intuitively, the identity writes the sigmoid as an average of bell-shaped curves
$e^{z/2 - \gamma z^2/2}$, one for every value of $\gamma$. In practice we replace
the expectation over $\gamma$ by a weighted sum over a few nodes $\gamma_j$ with
weights $w_j$ (Section~\ref{subsec:pg-quadrature}), so $\sigma$ becomes a finite
sum of weighted bells. Figure~\ref{fig:pcpg-bells} shows how the sum of the
bells with the largest weights approaches $\sigma(z)$: already four bells
reproduce the sigmoid closely, and with all nodes the difference is no longer
visible.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/pcpg/bells.pdf}
  \caption[The Pólya-Gamma identity as a sum of bells]{The Pólya-Gamma identity
    as a sum of bells. Partial sums of the weighted bells
    $\frac{1}{2} w_j e^{z/2 - \gamma_j z^2/2}$, with nodes $\gamma_j$ and weights
    $w_j$ from Golub--Welsch (Section~\ref{subsec:pg-quadrature}), largest weights
    first (light to dark), against $\sigma(z)$ (yellow). The first bell, the one with the largest
    weight, is decomposed further in Figure~\ref{fig:pcpg-waves}.}
  \label{fig:pcpg-bells}
\end{figure}

If we set $z := w^T x$, and insert into our expectation, we get:
\begin{align*}
  p(y \mid \xo)
  & = \E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)} \left[\sigma(z)\right] \\
  & = \E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)} \left[
       \frac{1}{2}\exp \left(\frac{1}{2}z\right)
       \E_{\gamma \sim \PG(1,0)} \left[\exp \left(-\frac{1}{2}\gamma z^2 \right)\right]
     \right] \\
  & = \frac{1}{2} \E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)} \E_{\gamma \sim \PG(1,0)}
    \left[ \exp \left(\frac{1}{2}z - \frac{1}{2}\gamma z^2 \right) \right]
\end{align*}

Now, we have a term without a sigmoid, but the $x$ still appears in a quadratic form.

\subsection{Trick 2: Hubbard-Stratonovich (HS)}

To get rid of the quadratic form, we apply the Hubbard--Stratonovich identity \cite{stratonovich1957, hubbard1959}, that states:

\begin{equation}
    \exp \left( -\tfrac{1}{2} a h^2 \right) = \sqrt{  \frac{1}{2 \pi a}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp \left(-\frac{u^2}{2a} -i h u\right) \mathrm{d}u,
\end{equation}

Since $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi a}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{u^2}{2a}\right)$ is the density of $\mathcal{N}(0,a)$, the integral is
exactly the expectation of $e^{-ihu}$ over $u \sim \mathcal{N}(0,a)$:
\begin{equation}
    \exp \left( -\tfrac{1}{2} a h^2 \right) = \E_{u \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\,a)}\!\left[ e^{-ihu} \right].
\end{equation}

In plain words, it makes a quadratic term linear at the cost of introducing a helper variable that we need to integrate over.

Currently, in our term, $x$ appears quadratically as well as linearly. To be able to apply HS, we complete the square (again using $z = w^T x$):

\begin{align}
  \frac{1}{2}z - \frac{1}{2}\gamma z^2
  &= -\frac{1}{2}\gamma \left(z^2 - \frac{z}{\gamma}\right) \notag\\
  &= -\frac{1}{2}\gamma \left(\left(z - \frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)^{2} - \frac{1}{4\gamma^2}\right) \notag\\
  &= -\frac{1}{2}\gamma \left(z - \frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)^{2} + \frac{1}{2}\gamma \cdot \frac{1}{4\gamma^2} \notag\\
  &= -\frac{1}{2}\gamma \left(z - \frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)^{2} + \frac{1}{8\gamma}.
\end{align}

Now we apply HS with $a = \gamma$ and $h = z - \frac{1}{2\gamma}$:

\begin{align}
  \exp \left(\frac{1}{2}z - \frac{1}{2}\gamma z^2 \right)
  &= \exp \left(\frac{1}{8\gamma}\right) \exp \left(-\frac{1}{2} \gamma \left(z - \frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)^{2} \right) \notag \\
  &= \exp \left(\frac{1}{8\gamma}\right) \sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi\gamma}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp \left(-\frac{u^2}{2\gamma} - i \left(z - \frac{1}{2\gamma}\right) u\right) \mathrm{d}u\notag \\
  &= \exp \left(\frac{1}{8\gamma}\right) \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\gamma}} \exp \left(-\frac{u^2}{2\gamma}\right)}_{\mathcal{N}(u; 0, \gamma)} \exp \left(-i \left(z - \frac{1}{2\gamma}\right) u \right) \mathrm{d}u \notag \\
  &= \exp \left(\frac{1}{8\gamma}\right) \E_{u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \gamma)} \left[ \exp \left(-i \left(z - \frac{1}{2\gamma}\right) u \right) \right] \notag \\
  &= \exp \left(\frac{1}{8\gamma}\right) \E_{u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \gamma)} \left[ \exp \left(\frac{iu}{2\gamma} - izu \right) \right]
\end{align}

Since $u$ and $-u$ are equally likely under $\mathcal{N}(0, \gamma)$, the
imaginary parts cancel, and with $c := \frac{1}{2\gamma}$ the identity reads
\[
  \exp \left(\frac{1}{2}z - \frac{1}{2}\gamma z^2 \right)
  = \exp \left(\frac{1}{8\gamma}\right) \E_{u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \gamma)} \left[ \cos \big(u (z - c)\big) \right].
\]
So every bell is an average of cosine waves centred at $c$, with frequencies
$u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \gamma)$. Again we replace the expectation by a weighted
sum, this time over Gauss--Hermite nodes (Section~\ref{subsec:gauss-hermite}).
Figure~\ref{fig:pcpg-waves} shows how the waves build up a bell: a single slow
wave is far too wide, but as faster waves are added, they pile up where the bell
is and cancel everywhere else.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/pcpg/waves.pdf}
  \caption[The Hubbard-Stratonovich identity as a sum of waves]{The
    Hubbard-Stratonovich identity as a sum of waves, for the bell with the
    largest weight in Figure~\ref{fig:pcpg-bells}. Partial sums of the weighted
    waves $e^{1/(8\gamma)} \frac{\omega_i}{\sqrt{\pi}} \cos(u_i(z - c))$, with
    Gauss--Hermite nodes $u_i = \sqrt{2\gamma}\, t_i$ and weights $\omega_i$

    together form one wave (light to dark), against the bell (blue).}
  \label{fig:pcpg-waves}
\end{figure}

Substituting back into $p(y \mid \xo)$, we get:

\begin{align*}
  p(y \mid \xo)
  &= \frac{1}{2} \E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)} \E_{\gamma \sim \PG(1,0)}
     \left[ \exp \left(\frac{1}{2}z - \frac{1}{2}\gamma z^2 \right) \right] \\
  &= \frac{1}{2} \E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)} \E_{\gamma \sim \PG(1,0)}
     \left[ \exp \left(\frac{1}{8\gamma}\right) \E_{u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \gamma)}
     \left[ \exp \left(\frac{iu}{2\gamma} - izu \right) \right] \right] \\
  &= \frac{1}{2} \E_{\gamma \sim \PG(1,0)} \E_{u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \gamma)}
     \left[ \exp \left(\frac{1 + 4iu}{8\gamma}\right)
     \E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)} \left[ \exp \left(-izu \right) \right] \right]
\end{align*}

\subsection{Characteristic Function}

The characteristic function of a random variable $X$ is defined as $\phi_X(s) = \E \left[ \exp(isX) \right]$.
The innermost expectation is only over $\xm$, with $\xo$ fixed, so we split $z = w^T x$ into its observed and missing parts:
\[
  z = w^T x = w_o^T \xo + w_m^T \xm.
\]
The $\xo$ part is constant under $\E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)}$ and factors out:
\begin{align*}
  \E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)} \left[ \exp(-izu) \right]
  &= \E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)} \left[ \exp\left(-i(w_o^T \xo + w_m^T \xm)u\right) \right] \\
  &= \exp\left(-iw_o^T \xo u\right) \cdot \E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)} \left[ \exp\left(-iw_m^T \xm u\right) \right] \\
  &= \exp\left(-iw_o^T \xo u\right) \cdot \phi_{\xm \mid \xo}(-uw_m)
\end{align*}
So the input to the characteristic function is $s := -uw_m$.

Figure~\ref{fig:pcpg-cf} shows what this buys us. Let $p(z)$ denote the
distribution of $z = w^T x$ under $p(\xm \mid \xo)$. For a fixed frequency $u$, the
expectation of a wave under $p(z)$ is the signed area under the wave times
$p(z)$. This area cannot be read off the density model directly, but it is
exactly a value of the characteristic function of $z$:
\[
  \E_{p(z)} \left[ \cos \big(u (z - c)\big) \right]
  = \mathrm{Re} \left[ e^{-iuc}\, \phi_z(u) \right],
  \qquad
  \phi_z(-u) = \exp\left(-iw_o^T \xo u\right) \cdot \phi_{\xm \mid \xo}(-uw_m),
\]
where the second equation is the one derived above. A slow wave hardly changes
over $p(z)$ and keeps most of the area, while a fast wave oscillates within
$p(z)$ and its positive and negative parts cancel; this is the decay of
$\phi_z$. Evaluating the characteristic function at all nodes $u_i$ therefore
gives the expectation of every wave, and this is the only place where the
density model is used.

  \label{fig:pcpg-cf}

\subsection{Full Formula}

Plugging the result of the previous subsection back into the outer
expression for $p(y \mid \xo)$, we obtain:
\begin{align*}
  p(y \mid \xo)
  &= \frac{1}{2} \E_{\gamma \sim \PG(1,0)} \E_{u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \gamma)}
     \left[ \exp \left(\frac{1 + 4iu}{8\gamma}\right)
     \cdot \exp\left(-iw_o^T \xo u\right) \cdot \phi_{\xm \mid \xo}(-uw_m) \right] \\
  &= \frac{1}{2} \E_{\gamma \sim \PG(1,0)} \E_{u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \gamma)}
     \left[ \exp \left(\frac{1}{8\gamma} + \frac{iu}{2\gamma} - iw_o^T \xo u\right)
     \cdot \phi_{\xm \mid \xo}(-uw_m) \right] \\
  &= \frac{1}{2} \E_{\gamma \sim \PG(1,0)} \E_{u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \gamma)}
     \left[ \exp \left(\frac{1}{8\gamma} + iu \left(\frac{1}{2\gamma} - w_o^T \xo\right)\right)
     \cdot \phi_{\xm \mid \xo}(-uw_m) \right]
\end{align*}

We propose this as a final expression that we can tractably evaluate.

Figure~\ref{fig:pcpg-summary} summarises the whole procedure. The expected
prediction $p(y \mid \xo)$ is the area under $\sigma(z)\, p(z)$. Each bell
contributes the area under the weighted bell times $p(z)$, which PCPG computes
from its waves and therefore from the characteristic function, and adding up
these areas rebuilds the expected prediction. Since every bell is positive, the
area only grows with each additional bell: the bell with the largest weight alone
covers less than half of it, four bells come close, and with all nodes the
estimate matches the exact value to four digits.

\subsection{A Direct Route for Gaussian Mixtures}
\label{subsec:gmm-direct}

If the density $p(x)$ is a Gaussian mixture model (GMM), the goal can be computed
directly, without either trick. We go through this in a few small steps.

\paragraph{Step 1: only one number matters.}
The prediction $\sigma(w^T x)$ does not look at the features one by one. It only
looks at the single number
\[
  z = w^T x = w_o^T \xo + w_m^T \xm.
\]
The observed part $w_o^T \xo$ is a fixed number. The missing part $w_m^T \xm$ is
uncertain, because $\xm$ is uncertain. So $z$ is a random number, and what we
want is the average of $\sigma(z)$.

\paragraph{Step 2: average over $z$ instead of over $\xm$.}
Many different values of $\xm$ lead to the same $z$, and therefore to the same
prediction. Instead of averaging $\sigma$ over all possible $\xm$, we can group
them by their value of $z$ and average over $z$, weighted by how likely each
value is:
\[
  \E_{p(\xm \mid \xo)} \left[ \sigma(z) \right] = \E_{z \sim p(z)} \left[ \sigma(z) \right].
\]
No matter how many features are missing, this is an average over a single
number. The only thing we need for it is the distribution $p(z)$.

\paragraph{Step 3: look at one mixture component at a time.}
A GMM describes the data as $K$ bell-shaped clusters, the mixture components,
numbered $k = 1, \dots, K$. One can think of a data point as being generated in
two steps: first a component $k$ is picked, then $x$ is drawn from that
component's Gaussian, with means $\mu_{kj}$ and variances $\sigma_{kj}^2$ for
each feature $j$. After seeing the observed features $\xo$, some components
become more plausible than others; $\tilde{\pi}_k(\xo)$ denotes the probability
that the point came from component $k$ (see Section~\ref{subsec:gmm-cf}).
Since the average over a mixture is the weighted average of the averages over
its components, we can handle each component on its own:
\[
  p(y \mid \xo) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \tilde{\pi}_k(\xo) \,
  \E \left[ \sigma(z) \mid k \right],
\]
where $\E[\sigma(z) \mid k]$ is the average prediction assuming the point came
from component $k$.

\paragraph{Step 4: within a component, $z$ is Gaussian.}
Assume the point came from component $k$. Then each missing feature $x_j$ is
Gaussian with mean $\mu_{kj}$ and variance $\sigma_{kj}^2$, and the missing
features are independent of each other. The number
\[
  z = w_o^T \xo + \sum_{j \text{ missing}} w_j x_j
\]
is a fixed number plus a weighted sum of independent Gaussians, and such a sum
is again Gaussian. Its mean and variance follow from two simple rules:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the mean of a weighted sum is the weighted sum of the means, so each
        missing feature is replaced by its mean in component $k$:
        \[ m_k = w_o^T \xo + \sum_{j \text{ missing}} w_j \, \mu_{kj}, \]
  \item multiplying $x_j$ by $w_j$ multiplies its variance by $w_j^2$, and
        variances of independent variables add up. The observed part is fixed
        and adds no variance:
        \[ v_k = \sum_{j \text{ missing}} w_j^2 \, \sigma_{kj}^2. \]
\end{itemize}
We write this as $z \mid k \sim \mathcal{N}(m_k, v_k)$, read as ``given that the
point came from component $k$, $z$ is normally distributed with mean $m_k$ and
variance $v_k$''. For example, with $z = 0.5 + 2 x_2 - x_3$, means
$(\mu_{k2}, \mu_{k3}) = (1, 0)$ and variances
$(\sigma_{k2}^2, \sigma_{k3}^2) = (1, 4)$, we get
$m_k = 0.5 + 2 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 0 = 2.5$ and
$v_k = 2^2 \cdot 1 + (-1)^2 \cdot 4 = 8$.

Together with Step 2, each component reduces to the average of the sigmoid
under a one-dimensional normal distribution:
\[
  p(y \mid \xo) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \tilde{\pi}_k(\xo) \,
  \E_{z \sim \mathcal{N}(m_k, v_k)} \left[ \sigma(z) \right].
\]

\paragraph{Step 5: compute each one-dimensional average.}
The average of the sigmoid under a normal distribution has no closed form, but
it is exactly the kind of integral Gauss--Hermite quadrature is made for
(Section~\ref{subsec:gauss-hermite}): evaluate $\sigma$ at a few fixed points
around the mean $m_k$, spread according to $\sqrt{v_k}$, and take a weighted sum.
This gives
\[
  p(y \mid \xo) \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \tilde{\pi}_k(\xo)
  \sum_{i=1}^{N} \omega_i \, \sigma \!\left( m_k + \sqrt{2 v_k}\, t_i \right).
\]

\paragraph{Why this does not work for a general PC.}
The route above needs two things: that we can go through the components one by
one (Step 3), and that $z$ is Gaussian within each of them (Step 4). A PC is
also a mixture, but the number of its components grows exponentially with its
size, so we cannot go through them one by one. And with non-Gaussian leaves, $z$
is not Gaussian, so its distribution is not known. What a PC can compute is the
average of a function that splits into one factor per feature, by pushing the
average down to the leaves. The sigmoid does not split this way. The
exponential $e^{-izu} = \prod_j e^{-i w_j x_j u}$ does, and its average is the
characteristic function. This is why, for general PCs, we rewrite the sigmoid
in terms of such exponentials, which leads to the full formula above.

\section{Implementation}

Two ingredients are still abstract in the formula above: the conditional
characteristic function $\phi_{\xm \mid \xo}$, which depends on the
density model, and the inner $\mathcal{N}(0, \gamma)$ expectation, which
needs to be approximated. This section makes both concrete.

\subsection{Conditional CF for a GMM}
\label{subsec:gmm-cf}

For a Gaussian mixture model $p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \,
\mathcal{N}(x; \mu_k, \Sigma_k)$ with diagonal covariances $\Sigma_k =
\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_k^2)$, the conditional density $p(\xm \mid \xo)$ is
again a mixture, with the same component shapes restricted to the missing
coordinates and reweighted by the posterior responsibility of each
component given $\xo$:
\[
  \tilde{\pi}_k(\xo) = \frac{\pi_k \, \mathcal{N}(\xo; \mu_k^o, \Sigma_k^o)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \pi_j \, \mathcal{N}(\xo; \mu_j^o, \Sigma_j^o)}.
\]
Since the characteristic function of $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$ at $s$ is
$\exp \left( i s^T \mu - \tfrac{1}{2} s^T \Sigma s \right)$, and the CF of
a mixture is the same convex combination of component CFs, we get a
closed-form expression:
\[
  \phi_{\xm \mid \xo}(s) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \tilde{\pi}_k(\xo) \,
  \exp \left( i s^T \mu_k^m - \tfrac{1}{2} s^T \Sigma_k^m s \right),
\]
where $\mu_k^m, \Sigma_k^m$ are the component means and covariances
restricted to the missing coordinates.

\subsection{Gauss--Hermite Quadrature}
\label{subsec:gauss-hermite}
The inner Gaussian expectation is amenable to Gauss--Hermite quadrature, with
deterministic nodes $\{t_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$ and weights $\{\omega_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$
such that

\[
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-t^2} g(t) \, \mathrm{d}t \approx \sum_{i=1}^{N} \omega_i \, g(t_i)
\]

To use this we need to bring the $\mathcal{N}(0, \gamma)$
expectation into the form $\int e^{-t^2} g(t)\,\mathrm{d}t$. Writing the
expectation out as an integral against the Gaussian density gives

\[
  \E_{u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \gamma)}[f(u)]
  = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\gamma}} \exp \!\left(-\frac{u^2}{2\gamma}\right) f(u) \, \mathrm{d}u.
\]

Apply the substitution $u = \sqrt{2\gamma}\,t$. It changes three pieces of
the integrand:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the differential: $\mathrm{d}u = \sqrt{2\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}t$,
  \item the Gaussian exponent: $\dfrac{u^2}{2\gamma} = \dfrac{(\sqrt{2\gamma}\,t)^2}{2\gamma} = \dfrac{2\gamma\, t^2}{2\gamma} = t^2$,
  \item the argument of $f$: $f(u) = f \!\left(\sqrt{2\gamma}\,t\right)$,
\end{itemize}
and the integration limits are unchanged since $\sqrt{2\gamma} > 0$. Plugging
all three substitutions into the integral:
\begin{align*}
  \E_{u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \gamma)}[f(u)]
  &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\gamma}} \, e^{-t^2} f \!\left(\sqrt{2\gamma}\,t\right) \sqrt{2\gamma} \, \mathrm{d}t \\
  &= \frac{\sqrt{2\gamma}}{\sqrt{2\pi\gamma}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-t^2} f \!\left(\sqrt{2\gamma}\,t\right) \mathrm{d}t.
\end{align*}
The constant prefactor simplifies to
\[
  \frac{\sqrt{2\gamma}}{\sqrt{2\pi\gamma}}
  = \sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{2\pi\gamma}}
  = \sqrt{\frac{1}{\pi}}
  = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}},
\]
so the Gaussian expectation becomes
\[
  \E_{u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \gamma)}[f(u)]
  = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-t^2} f \!\left(\sqrt{2\gamma}\,t\right) \mathrm{d}t.
\]

Which we then can apply by
\[
  \E_{u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \gamma)}[f(u)]
  \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \omega_i\, f \!\left(\sqrt{2\gamma}\,t_i\right).
\]

Plugging this into the inner expectation of our final formula, with\\
\[
  f(u) = \exp \!\left( \frac{1}{8\gamma} + i u \left(\frac{1}{2\gamma} - w_o^T \xo \right) \right) \phi_{\xm \mid \xo}(-u w_m)
\]
gives

\[
  p(y \mid \xo) \approx
  \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}} \E_{\gamma \sim \PG(1,0)} \left[
    \sum_{i=1}^{N} \omega_i \,
    \exp \!\left( \frac{1}{8\gamma} + i \sqrt{2\gamma}\, t_i \left(\frac{1}{2\gamma} - w_o^T \xo \right) \right)
    \phi_{\xm \mid \xo}\!\left(-\sqrt{2\gamma}\, t_i\, w_m\right)
  \right]
\]

\subsection{Approximating the PG integral}
\label{subsec:pg-quadrature}

We also want don't want to sample the outer expectation over $\gamma \sim \PG(1,0)$ and
construct a set of fixed nodes and weights that "approximate" the PG(1,0) measure best.
From what we found, there are multiple ways to do this.

All of them replace the expectation by a weighted sum,
$\E_{\gamma \sim \PG(1,0)}[f(\gamma)] \approx \sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j f(\gamma_j)$, with
nodes $\gamma_j > 0$ and weights $w_j \geq 0$ summing to one. To see what such a
rule has to achieve, note that the function of $\gamma$ in the PG identity is
$e^{-\gamma z^2/2}$, and its expectation is the Laplace transform of $\PG(1,0)$,
$\mathcal{L}(t) := \E[e^{-t\gamma}] = \operatorname{sech}(\sqrt{t/2})$, evaluated at
$t = z^2/2$. The PG identity can therefore be written as
\begin{equation}
  \sigma(z) = \tfrac{1}{2}\, e^{z/2}\, \mathcal{L}\!\left(\tfrac{z^2}{2}\right),
  \qquad
  \hat\sigma(z) = \tfrac{1}{2}\, e^{z/2} \sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j\, e^{-\gamma_j z^2/2},
  \label{eq:sigma-laplace}
\end{equation}
where $\hat\sigma$ is what a rule with $n$ nodes produces. Every rule below is a
different way of making $\sum_j w_j e^{-\gamma_j t}$ close to $\mathcal{L}(t)$.
For large $|z|$ this is hard: $e^{-\gamma z^2/2}$ is then only non-negligible for
very small $\gamma$, so the rules differ mainly in how well they cover the region
near $\gamma = 0$.

\subsubsection{Golub--Welsch construction}
One is the \emph{Golub--Welsch algorithm}
\cite{golubwelsch1969}, which produces $n$-point quadrature nodes and weights
from the first $2n$ moments of any positive measure.

The Laplace transform of $\PG(1,0)$ is known in closed form:
\[
  \mathcal{L}(t)
  := \E_{\gamma \sim \PG(1,0)}\!\left[e^{-t\gamma}\right]
  = \operatorname{sech}\!\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{t}{2}}\right)
  = \frac{1}{\cosh\!\left(\sqrt{t/2}\right)}.
\]
The raw moments follow from derivatives at zero:
\[
  \mu_k := \E[\gamma^k] = (-1)^k\, \mathcal{L}^{(k)}(0),
\]
which can be evaluated numerically to arbitrary precision.

Given $\mu_0, \dots, \mu_{2n-1}$, the algorithm goes as follows.
Form the $n \times n$ Hankel moment matrices
\[
  H_{ij} = \mu_{i+j}, \qquad H'_{ij} = \mu_{i+j+1}, \quad i,j = 0,\dots,n-1.
\]
Compute the Cholesky factor $H = R^\top R$ and the Jacobi (tridiagonal) matrix
\[
  J = R^{-\top} H' R^{-1}, \quad \text{symmetrised as } J \leftarrow \tfrac{1}{2}(J + J^\top).
\]
Eigendecompose $J = V \Lambda V^\top$.  Then the quadrature nodes and weights are
\[
  \gamma_j = \lambda_j, \qquad w_j = \mu_0\, v_{0j}^2, \quad j = 1,\dots,n,
\]
where $\lambda_j$ are the eigenvalues and $v_{0j}$ is the first component of
the $j$-th eigenvector.  The resulting rule integrates polynomials in $\gamma$
exactly up to degree $2n-1$.

The Hankel matrix is extremely ill-conditioned, so the whole computation is done
in arbitrary precision (200 digits). Even then, the rule is only reliable up to
$n \approx 19$.

\subsubsection{Log-trapezoid rule}
Substituting $\gamma = e^s$ turns the expectation into
\[
  \E_{\gamma \sim \PG(1,0)}[f(\gamma)] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(e^s)\, p(e^s)\, e^s \,\mathrm{d}s,
\]
where $p$ is the density of $\PG(1,0)$. Applying the trapezoid rule with $n$
equally spaced nodes $s_j$ and spacing $h$ gives nodes $\gamma_j = e^{s_j}$ and
weights $w_j \propto p(\gamma_j)\,\gamma_j\,h$. The density has no closed form, but
two alternating series, one converging fast for small and one for large
$\gamma$, evaluate it to machine precision. Since $p$ decays like $e^{-1/(8\gamma)}$
towards $0$ and like $e^{-\pi^2\gamma/2}$ towards infinity, the range
$\gamma \in [10^{-3}, 10]$ covers all of the mass. Equal spacing in $\log\gamma$
places many nodes at small $\gamma$, exactly where $e^{-\gamma z^2/2}$ lives for
large $|z|$, and for smooth integrands that vanish at both ends the trapezoid rule
converges exponentially in $n$. Unlike Golub--Welsch, it works for any $n$.

\subsubsection{Quantile rule}
The simplest deterministic rule places the nodes at the quantiles of $\PG(1,0)$,
\[
  \gamma_j = F^{-1}\!\left(\frac{j - \tfrac{1}{2}}{n}\right), \qquad w_j = \frac{1}{n},
\]
with $F$ the CDF of $\PG(1,0)$, again computed from a series. This is the
midpoint rule in probability space, i.e.\ one-dimensional quasi-Monte Carlo:
the same equal weights as MC, but with the nodes spread evenly instead of drawn at
random. Its error decays roughly like $1/n^2$. We include it mainly as a baseline
between MC and the more elaborate rules.

\subsubsection{Nonnegative least-squares construction}
The Golub--Welsch rule matches the \emph{moments} of $\PG(1,0)$, i.e.\ the
Taylor coefficients of $\mathcal{L}$ at $t=0$, so its accuracy is concentrated
near $z=0$ and decays as $|z|$ grows. An alternative is to match the Laplace
transform directly across the range of $z$.

Fix $M$ candidate support locations $\{\gamma_j\}_{j=1}^M$ log-spaced in
$[\gamma_{\min}, \gamma_{\max}]$, and $R \gg M$ fitting points $\{z_r\}_{r=1}^R$
linearly spaced in $[0, z_{\max}]$. Define
\[
  A_{rj} = e^{-z_r \gamma_j}, \qquad
  b_r = \mathcal{L}(z_r) = \operatorname{sech}\!\left(\sqrt{z_r/2}\right),
\]
and solve the nonnegative least-squares problem
\[
  \min_{w \ge 0} \; \|A w - b\|_2^2.
\]
The solution $w$ is then normalized to sum to one, and support points with
$w_j < \varepsilon$ are eliminated. The remaining pairs $(\gamma_j, w_j)$ serve
as quadrature nodes and weights for the outer PG integral. NNLS solutions are
sparse, so only around a dozen of the $M$ candidates are used, and the number of
nodes is not chosen directly.

The fit only constrains $\mathcal{L}$ for $t \leq z_{\max}$, which corresponds to
$|z| \leq \sqrt{2 z_{\max}}$. If the candidate grid reaches very small $\gamma$
(e.g.\ $10^{-5}$), the fit puts weight on a node whose exponential is almost
constant on that range, because this helps to match the slowly decaying tail of
$\mathcal{L}$. Outside the fitted range this constant does not decay, and through
the factor $e^{z/2}$ in \eqref{eq:sigma-laplace} the estimate of $\sigma(z)$ grows
without bound. We therefore start the grid at $\gamma_{\min} = 10^{-2}$.

\subsubsection{Fully deterministic formula.}
Combining PG nodes $(\gamma_j, w_j)_{j=1}^{M}$ with Gauss--Hermite
nodes $(t_i, \omega_i)_{i=1}^{N}$ yields the deterministic approximation
\[
  p(y \mid \xo) \approx
  \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}} \sum_{j=1}^{M} w_j \sum_{i=1}^{N} \omega_i \,
  \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{8\gamma_j} + i\sqrt{2\gamma_j}\,t_i
    \left(\frac{1}{2\gamma_j} - w_o^T \xo\right)\right)
  \phi_{\xm \mid \xo}\!\left(-\sqrt{2\gamma_j}\,t_i\,w_m\right).
\]

\subsection{Sign Flip}
\label{subsec:sign-flip}

\subsubsection{Why large positive $z$ is hard}
In \eqref{eq:sigma-laplace}, any error $\varepsilon$ a rule makes in approximating
$\mathcal{L}(z^2/2)$ enters the estimate of $\sigma(z)$ multiplied by
$\tfrac{1}{2}e^{z/2}$. The nodes only see $z^2$, so this error is the same for $z$
and $-z$, but its effect is not:
\[
  \bigl|\hat\sigma(z) - \sigma(z)\bigr| = \tfrac{1}{2}\, e^{z/2}\, \varepsilon(z^2/2).
\]
For $z < 0$ the factor shrinks the error, for $z > 0$ it amplifies it
exponentially. Staying at $\sigma(z) \approx 1$ for large positive $z$ requires the
sum of exponentials to reproduce the tail $\mathcal{L}(z^2/2) \approx 2e^{-z/2}$
almost exactly, which only rules with nodes very close to $\gamma = 0$ can do.
Rules without such nodes (Golub--Welsch, quantile) have a tail that is too small,
so $\hat\sigma(z)$ falls back to $0$; a rule with too much weight near $0$ (NNLS
with $\gamma_{\min} = 10^{-5}$) has a tail that is too heavy, so $\hat\sigma(z)$
blows up. Only the log-trapezoid rule stays accurate without further help, and
only with enough nodes.

\subsubsection{The flip}
The sigmoid is point symmetric around $(0, \tfrac{1}{2})$:
\[
  \sigma(-z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{z}} = \frac{e^{-z}}{e^{-z} + 1} = 1 - \sigma(z).
\]
For $z > 0$ we can therefore compute $1 - \hat\sigma(-z)$ instead of $\hat\sigma(z)$.
Both use the same nodes and the same approximation of $\mathcal{L}(z^2/2)$; only
the factor multiplying its error changes from $e^{z/2}$ to $e^{-z/2}$. The error
at $z$ becomes the error at $-z$, which is small for every rule. In the
one-dimensional identity this reduces the maximum error over $|z| \leq 30$ of
Golub--Welsch with 19 nodes from about $1$ to $6.5 \cdot 10^{-7}$, and of the quantile
rule with 15 nodes from about $1$ to $4 \cdot 10^{-4}$.

The identity holds for the whole expectation as well,
\[
  \E\bigl[\sigma(w^T x) \mid \xo\bigr] = 1 - \E\bigl[\sigma(-w^T x) \mid \xo\bigr],
\]
so the estimator can run with $-w$ and return one minus the result. Since $z = w^T x$
is random, the flip is decided by the sign of its conditional mean
$\E[w^T x \mid \xo] = w_o^T \xo + w_m^T \E[\xm \mid \xo]$, which is the expectation
of a linear function and therefore cheap for a GMM as well as for a PC. Part of the
distribution of $z$ can still lie on the positive side, but close to $0$, where
the amplification is moderate. For a GMM, where $z$ is Gaussian within each
component, the flip can also be applied per component.

\subsubsection{Fitting the nodes to the sigmoid}
\label{subsubsec:sigmoid-fit}
With the flip, the rule is only evaluated for $z$ around and below $0$, and the
error that matters is $\tfrac{1}{2}e^{z/2}\varepsilon(z^2/2)$ on that side. None of the
rules above optimise this quantity: Golub--Welsch matches moments, the
log-trapezoid and quantile rules discretise the PG distribution, and NNLS matches
$\mathcal{L}$ with equal weight on all $t \leq z_{\max}$.

Since the rule enters the estimator linearly, the estimator computes exactly
$\E[\hat\sigma(z)]$, and its error is the error of $\hat\sigma$ averaged over the
distribution of $z$. Nothing in the rest of the method requires the nodes to
represent $\PG(1,0)$: HS and the characteristic function only need $\gamma_j > 0$,
and the weights only need to be nonnegative. We can therefore choose the nodes
and weights to approximate the sigmoid itself,
\[
  \min_{w \geq 0} \sum_{r} \Bigl( \tfrac{1}{2}\, e^{z_r/2} \sum_{j=1}^{M} w_j\, e^{-\gamma_j z_r^2/2} - \sigma(z_r) \Bigr)^2,
\]
over a log-spaced candidate grid $\gamma_j$ and fitting points $z_r \in [-30, 5]$,
again solved with NNLS. This is the NNLS fit of $\mathcal{L}$ from above, but
with the errors weighted by $\tfrac{1}{2}e^{z/2}$, i.e.\ by exactly how much they
affect the sigmoid. The margin up to $z = 5$ covers the part of the distribution
of $z$ that remains on the positive side after the flip. The grid starts at
$\gamma_{\min} = 0.02$, so that $e^{1/(8\gamma)}$ in the inner integral stays below
about $500$. The resulting rule has about 12 nodes and a maximum error of about
$10^{-8}$ on $[-30, 5]$, roughly a hundred times smaller than Golub--Welsch with
15 nodes.

This changes the view of the outer integral: the method approximates the
sigmoid by functions of the form $\tfrac{1}{2}e^{z/2}e^{-\gamma z^2/2}$, whose
expectations are tractable, and the PG identity is one particular, exact but
infinite, choice of how to combine them. A finite rule only has to combine them
well, not to represent the PG distribution. The only variants this does not apply
to are the ones that sample $\gamma$ from $\PG(1,0)$, since a fitted rule cannot
be sampled from.

\subsection{Algorithms}

Putting the pieces together, we can define three variants of our method, depending on how we want to 
approximate the integrals. A full Monte Carlo version samples both $\gamma_j$ and $u_i$; 
a Gauss--Hermite version samples only $\gamma_j$ and uses quadrature for $u_i$; 
and a fully deterministic version uses quadrature for both.

\subsubsection{Full Monte Carlo}

Below is an algorithm for the simplest - fully Monte Carlo - variant.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{PCPG: expected sigmoid under a GMM, full Monte Carlo}
\label{alg:pcpg-mc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require LR weights $w$, partial observation $\xo$,
GMM parameters $\{\pi_k, \mu_k, \Sigma_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$,
\# PG samples $M$, \# Gaussian samples $N$
\State Split $w = (w_o, w_m)$ and $\mu_k = (\mu_k^o, \mu_k^m)$, $\Sigma_k = (\Sigma_k^o, \Sigma_k^m)$ by the observed/missing mask
\State $\tilde{\pi}_k \gets \dfrac{\pi_k \, \mathcal{N}(\xo;\mu_k^o,\Sigma_k^o)}{\sum_{j}\pi_j \,\mathcal{N}(\xo;\mu_j^o,\Sigma_j^o)}$ \hfill for $k=1,\dots,K$
\State $c \gets w_o^T \xo$
\State $S \gets 0$
\For{$j = 1, \dots, M$}
  \State Sample $\gamma_j \sim \PG(1, 0)$
  \For{$i = 1, \dots, N$}
    \State Sample $u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \gamma_j)$
    \State $s \gets -u\, w_m$
    \State $\phi \gets \sum_{k=1}^{K} \tilde{\pi}_k \exp \!\left( i s^T \mu_k^m - \tfrac{1}{2} s^T \Sigma_k^m s \right)$
    \State $\mathrm{factor} \gets \exp \!\left( \dfrac{1}{8\gamma_j} + i u \left( \dfrac{1}{2\gamma_j} - c \right) \right)$
    \State $S \gets S + \mathrm{factor} \cdot \phi$
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return $\dfrac{1}{2\,M\,N} \cdot \mathrm{Re}(S)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{PCPG (unified)}
\label{alg:pcpg}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require LR weights $w$, partial observation $\xo$,
GMM parameters $\{\pi_k, \mu_k, \Sigma_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$,
outer iterates $(\gamma_j, w_j)_{j=1}^{M}$, inner iterates $(u_i, \omega_i)_{i=1}^{N}$ (per $\gamma_j$), normalisation $Z$
\State Split $w = (w_o, w_m)$ by the observed/missing mask
\State $\tilde{\pi}_k \gets \dfrac{\pi_k \, \mathcal{N}(\xo;\mu_k^o,\Sigma_k^o)}{\sum_{j}\pi_j \,\mathcal{N}(\xo;\mu_j^o,\Sigma_j^o)}$ \hfill for $k=1,\dots,K$
\State $c \gets w_o^T \xo$,\quad $S \gets 0$
\For{$j = 1, \dots, M$}
  \For{$i = 1, \dots, N$}
    \State $s \gets -u_i\, w_m$
    \State $\phi \gets \sum_{k} \tilde{\pi}_k \exp \!\left( i s^T \mu_k^m - \tfrac{1}{2} s^T \Sigma_k^m s \right)$
    \State $\mathrm{factor} \gets \exp \!\left( \dfrac{1}{8\gamma_j} + i u_i \left( \dfrac{1}{2\gamma_j} - c \right) \right)$
    \State $S \gets S + w_j\, \omega_i\, \mathrm{factor} \cdot \phi$
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return $Z \cdot \mathrm{Re}(S)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Of course one could also add a forth variant that uses quadrature for the PG integral but sampling for the inner Gaussian, but we don't include it here since it doesn't perform well in practice. 
The variance of the inner Gaussian sampling is very high which makes the quadrature perform much better.
